\begin{abstract}

Sea-level Projections from Empirical Calibration of Thermodynamical, Reduced-physics Models (SPECTRUM)

Every centimeter of sea-level rise exposes millions more people to annual coastal flooding, yet projections of sea-level come with large uncertainties. Many of the commonly used projections rely on complex models calibrated on data too sparse to adequately constrain them, causing the outputs to depend on model assumptions in ways that remain unquantified and nontransparent. Here, we construct a data-driven sea-level forecast by calibrating parsimonious physical models for individual sea-level contributors against decades-long independent observational records. When forced with observed surface temperatures, the calibrated components sum to match observed global mean sea level rise (GMSLR) from satellite altimetry collected since 1993. Hindcasts match GMSLR reconstructions within error to at least 1950, or approximately 1 $\circ$C warming. Our forecasts show a 58\% probability of GMSLR exceeding 1 meter, and 5\% of exceeding 2 meters, by the end of the century. The dynamics of West Antarctic glaciers account for $\sim90$\% of the forecast uncertainty and reveal orthogonal dimensions of sea-level risk: emissions reduction decreases median projections related to the predictable, temperature-sensitive components, but leaves the high-risk tail of the distribution largely unchanged, exposing hundreds of millions of people to accelerating sea-level risks.

\end{abstract}

\subsubsection{Greenland Ice Sheet}

The Greenland Ice Sheet (GrIS) is the largest land-ice contributor and has lost mass every year since the 1990s \citep{Mouginot2019,Mankoff2021}. That loss is the imbalance between solid-ice discharge and surface mass balance (SMB), where SMB is the difference between mass gained by snow accumulation and mass lost to melting. 

GrIS discharge is sensitive to ocean temperature and surface melt runoff \citep{Straneo2013,Slater2020,Wood2021}. Warm, subsurface waters thin and undercut the calving fronts of marine-terminating glaciers, driving increased calving and net terminus retreat. This effect is amplified where surface runoff drives mixing and buoyant upwelling along the calving face \citep{Slater2022}. Surface melt also lubricates glacier beds, but that effect is generally short-lived and localized as the subglacial hydrological system evolves to accommodate changes in meltwater flux \citep{Schoof2010,Tedstone2013,Ultee2022}.
 
Focusing on the strong influence of ocean-induced melting on discharge, we approximate the GrIS-wide discharge as
\begin{equation}
	D = \gamma\,T_{\mathrm{ocean}}(t - \delta) + r_0,
	\label{eq:greenland_rate}
\end{equation}
where $D$ is the GrIS discharge in sea-level equivalent units, $\gamma$ is an effective discharge sensitivity to ocean temperature, $\delta$ is the lag between distal ocean warming and ice sheet response, $r_0$ is the background discharge rate at the baseline ocean temperature, and $T_{\mathrm{ocean}}$ is the ocean temperature anomaly in the ocean waters around Greenland relative to the 1995--2005 mean. Because $D$ is the gross discharge flux rather than an anomaly, it becomes a contribution to GMSL only when combined with SMB, whose accumulation term offsets most of the background rate $r_0$. We calibrate against total GrIS discharge, integrated across the 219 marine-terminating outlet glaciers resolved in the observational record \citep{Mouginot2019} to trade spatial resolution for signal-to-noise in the data. Our goal is to capture ice-sheet scale discharge rather than the individual basins and glaciers. 

The calibration is carried out on the time-integrated form of Eq.~\ref{eq:greenland_rate}, which gives the
cumulative contribution of GrIS discharge to GMSLR,
	\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:greenland_dyn}
      H_{\mathrm{D}}(t) = \int_{t_0}^{t} D dt',
	\end{equation}
with $\gamma$ and $r_0$ set constant in time. Fitting the time-integrated (cumulative) record further improves the signal-to-noise ratio, because the cumulative signal grows linearly in time while the annual errors accumulate only as its square root. It also directly constrains the total mass lost, the quantity of interest in this study.

The calibration uses 47 years of glacier discharge data \citep{Mouginot2019} and ocean temperatures from EN4 (v4.2.2, g10 bias correction) \citep{Good2013}, averaged over 200--500\,m depth, the range most relevant to Greenland's tidewater grounding lines \citep{Slater2020}, in the seas surrounding Greenland south of 80$^\circ$N (58--80$^\circ$N, 75--5$^\circ$W). This geographic limit is set by the sparse subsurface sampling farther north (Fig. \ref{fig:greenland_delay_fit}). Because the data are resolved annually, the lag $\delta$ is in integer-year values. We then fit $(\gamma, r_0)$ by weighted least squares separately for a range of $\delta$ values, using the demeaned cumulative record. Each fit carries a penalty on the mismatch between the modeled discharge rate at the end of the time-series and the rate observed over the final decade, which keeps the calibration consistent with the present-day discharge rate. The posterior is then formed as a Gaussian mixture of the fits for each $\delta$, with weights derived from the Bayesian Information Criterion. This procedure places 99.5\% of the weight on $\delta = 5$~yr and gives posterior medians $\gamma = 0.365$~mm SLE yr$^{-1}\,^\circ$C$^{-1}$ (0.312--0.419~mm\,SLE\,yr$^{-1}\,^\circ$C$^{-1}$) and $r_0 = 1.383$~mm\,SLE\,yr$^{-1}$ (1.367--1.399~mm\,SLE\,yr$^{-1}$). The calibration target is a cumulative record, resulting in correlated errors, so we report parameter intervals from a covariance that accounts for that correlation and for the excess scatter of the residuals relative to the reported observational errors, such that reduced chi-square $\chi^2/\mathrm{dof} = 1.8$, where $\chi^2$ is the error-weighted sum of squared residuals and $\mathrm{dof} = 45$ is the number of annual observations (47) less the two fitted parameters. We validate the model against a discharge time-series spanning 1986--2023 that was withheld from calibration \citep{Mankoff2021} and the time-series from the Ice Sheet Mass Balance Intercomparison Exercise (IMBIE-3) spanning 1972--2023 \citep{Otosaka2026}. The validation with \citet{Mankoff2021} is informative but weak because the \citet{Mouginot2019} calibration and \citet{Mankoff2021} time-series share the same underlying raw data and with independent processing methodology. The IMBIE-3 time-series is a combination of 42 independent mass balance measurements from satellite observations of glacier flow, surface elevation, and gravitational anomaly. Our model falls within error of the validation data for discharge and total mass balance (Fig. \ref{fig:greenland_delay_fit}). The validation results suggest that our simple model, with two fitted parameters and a selected lag, captures the total contribution of GrIS discharge to GMSLR during the observational record and its sensitivity to ocean temperatures in Greenland's peripheral waters.

The fitted $\delta = 5$~yr is an effective lag for spatially averaged forcing and we therefore do not attempt to attribute it to a single process. It lies within the range of timescales spanned by the ocean-to-ice pathway, from exchange between the continental shelf and fjord interiors through the terminus response to undercutting and the inland transmission of retreat to the flux gates at which discharge is measured \citep{StraneoHeimbach2013,Wood2021,robel_response_2018}. Because $T_{\mathrm{ocean}}$ is averaged over Greenland's peripheral waters, $\delta$ also absorbs the time required for a warming anomaly to become representative of conditions at outlet glaciers distributed around the ice sheet. 

To our knowledge, no published study reports a Greenland-wide sensitivity of discharge rate to ocean temperature, so we derive a simple dimensional scaling to contextualize the inferred value $\gamma = 0.365$~mm\,SLE\,yr$^{-1}$\,$^\circ$C$^{-1}$. For a single outlet glacier $i$, the submarine melt rate scales approximately linearly with ocean thermal forcing, the ocean temperature above the in situ freezing point $T_{\mathrm{ocean}}$, such that
\begin{equation}
	\Delta \dot{m}_i = \kappa_i T_{\mathrm{ocean}},
	\label{eq:melt_rate}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa_i$ is the melt sensitivity to thermal forcing averaged over the submerged calving face. Plume parameterizations calibrated against Greenland observations \citep{Xu2013, Rignot2016, Slater2022} give a maximum melt sensitivity near the grounding line of 90--260~m\,yr$^{-1}$\,$^\circ$C$^{-1}$. The face-averaged rate is two to three times smaller \citep{Wood2021}. We therefore take $\kappa = 36$--$104$~m\,yr$^{-1}$\,$^\circ$C$^{-1}$, with median 60~m\,yr$^{-1}$\,$^\circ$C$^{-1}$. Submarine melt thins and undercuts the terminus, triggering calving and grounding-line retreat so that the total ice flux perturbation is a factor $\mu$ times the direct submarine melt flux. For Greenland's tidewater glaciers, observations and models suggest $\mu = 3$--$10$ \citep{Enderlin2013, Wood2021}. The flux perturbation from a single glacier with submerged calving-front area $A_f$ is then
\begin{equation}
	\Delta \dot{Q}_i = \mu_i\,\rho_{ice}\,A_{f,i}\,\kappa_i T_{\mathrm{ocean}},
	\label{eq:single_glacier_flux}
\end{equation}
where $\rho_{ice} = 900$~kg\,m$^{-3}$ is the mass density of ice. Summing over Greenland's $N = 219$ marine-terminating outlets \citep{Mouginot2019} we approximate the scale of $\gamma$ as
\begin{equation}
	\widetilde{\gamma} \sim \frac{1}{A_{\mathrm{ocean}}\,\rho_{fw} T_{\mathrm{ocean}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \Delta\dot{Q}_i ,
	\label{eq:gamma_aggregate}
\end{equation}
where $A_{\mathrm{ocean}} = 3.625 \times 10^{14}$~m$^2$ is the ocean area and $\rho_{fw} = 1000$~kg\,m$^{-3}$ is the density of freshwater, which converts added ice mass to sea-level equivalent under the convention 1~mm\,SLE~$= 362.5$~Gt used throughout this study. Submerged calving-front areas of 0.9--2.7~km$^2$ are reported for five West Greenland outlets \citep{Rignot2016}. Evaluating Eq.~\ref{eq:gamma_aggregate} across the full parameter ranges gives
\begin{equation}
	\widetilde{\gamma} = 0.05\text{--}1.5~\mathrm{mm\,SLE\,yr}^{-1}\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}^{-1},
	\label{eq:gamma_estimate}
\end{equation}
which contains our calibrated $\gamma = 0.365$~mm\,SLE\,yr$^{-1}$\,$^\circ$C$^{-1}$. We report Eq.~\ref{eq:gamma_estimate} as a rough order-of-magnitude check on the inferred value intended for intuition building. The range spans a factor of 30 because it is the product of four quantities that are not measured in aggregate, and the factorization $\sum_i \mu_i A_{f,i} \kappa_i \approx N\,\bar{\mu}\,\bar{A}_f\,\bar{\kappa}$ assumes these are uncorrelated across outlets, whereas the deepest and widest fronts occupy the warmest fjords. Finally, the calibrated $\gamma$ is an effective sensitivity that absorbs the covarying atmospheric pathway because increased subglacial runoff amplifies plume-driven melting alongside ocean warming. 



\subsubsection{Thermosteric}

The ocean is the dominant reservoir for the excess heat accumulated by the climate system \citep{Cheng2017}, and the resulting expansion -- the thermosteric contribution -- is currently the largest single contributor to observed sea level rise \citep{Frederikse2020}. Both heat exchange between ocean and atmosphere and ocean dynamics set the structure of a simple model of the thermosteric effect. Put simply, the ocean warms with a delay relative to the atmosphere, heat enters at the sea surface and is carried downward by turbulent mixing and by the overturning circulation, causing temperature of the interior to reflect the history of surface warming. Downward transfer spans a wide range of timescales. The upper few hundred meters exchange heat with the atmosphere over years to decades, whereas water below the thermocline is renewed over centuries. We therefore represent the ocean as two layers, a fast upper layer coupled to the atmosphere and a slow deep layer coupled only to the upper layer, following the structure of \citet{Geoffroy2013}. This structure reproduces both the lag between surface warming and expansion and the continued expansion that follows a stabilization of surface temperature, where the deep layer draws heat from the upper layer after the upper layer has equilibrated.

Let us first consider an upper layer of thickness $h_u=700$ m warmed by $\Delta\theta(z,t)$, which raises steric height of the ocean over time as
	\begin{equation}
	H_{\mathrm{u}} = \int_0^{h_u} \alpha_{sw} \Delta\theta\,dz,
	\label{eq:steric_integral}
	\end{equation}
where $\alpha_{sw}(z)$ is the thermal expansion coefficient of seawater, which is depth dependent due to its temperature dependence. The temperature anomaly profile is defined as $\Delta\theta = S_u\varphi$, where $S_u(t)$ is the amplitude of the anomaly and $\varphi(z)$ is a dimensionless shape factor. Expanding $\alpha_{sw}(z) = \alpha_{0_{sw}}(z) + \alpha'_{sw}\Delta\theta$, with $\alpha'_{sw} = \partial\alpha_{sw}/\partial\theta$, we rewrite Eq. \ref{eq:steric_integral} as 
	\begin{equation}
	H_{\mathrm{u}} = a_u S_u^2 + b_u S_u + \epsilon_u(t - t_0),
	\label{eq:thermo_upper}
	\end{equation}
where $a_u = \int_0^{h_u} \alpha'_{sw}\varphi^2dz$ and $b_u = \int_0^{h_u} \alpha_{0_{sw}}\varphi dz$. The linear term $b_u$ is the expansion of the upper layer for a given warming while the quadratic term $a_u$ comes from the temperature dependence of $\alpha_{sw}$. We tacked on $\epsilon_u(t - t_0)$ to absorb instrument drift during calibration. As GMST, $T(t)$, changes, the amplitude evolves as
	\begin{equation}
	\frac{dS_u}{dt} = \frac{T - S_u}{\tau_u},
	\label{eq:thermo_su}
	\end{equation}
where $\tau_u$ is the characteristic time over which the thermal state $S_u$ relaxes to GMST. Equation~\ref{eq:thermo_su} shows that $S_u$ is an average of past temperatures, weighted toward the recent past over a time window set by $\tau_u$. The upper layer therefore smooths year-to-year fluctuations in GMST and follows longer trends with a delay comparable to $\tau_u$. 

The deep layer follows the same construction, with $\Delta\theta_d(z,t) = S_d(t)\,\varphi_d(z)$ over 700--2000 m depth and $b_d = \int_{h_u}^{h_d} \alpha_{0_{sw}}\,\varphi_d\,dz$, with $h_d=2000$ m. We drop the quadratic term because $S_d$ remains small enough that the temperature dependence of $\alpha_{sw}$ can be neglected. Warming from GMST reaches the deep layer only after passing through the upper layer, so the thermal state $S_d$ relaxes toward $S_u$ as
      \begin{equation}
      \frac{dS_d}{dt} = \frac{S_u - S_d}{\tau_d},
      \label{eq:thermo_sd}
      \end{equation}
where we fixed $\tau_d = 150$ yr at the CMIP5 multi-model mean deep-ocean adjustment time
\citep[][Table 4]{Geoffroy2013}, because the 21-year 0--2000\,m data record is too short to constrain a centennial relaxation time. Adding this layer gives the total steric response for the upper 2000 m as,
      \begin{equation}
      H_{ud}(t) = H_{\mathrm{u}}(t) + b_d\,S_d,
      \label{eq:thermo_total}
      \end{equation}
which we fit jointly to the 0--700\,m and 0--2000 m records. Finally, to account for expansion below 2000 m depth, we define 
	\begin{equation}
	H_{\mathrm{full}}(t) = H_{ud}(t) + \dot{H}_{b}(t - t_{\mathrm{ref}}),
	\label{eq:thermo_full}
	\end{equation}
where $\dot{H}_{b} = 0.07 \pm 0.04$\,mm\,yr$^{-1}$ \citep{Purkey2010} is a constant rate for depths below 2000\,m, referenced to year $t_{\mathrm{ref}} = 2000$. This layer is not observed at annual resolution, so its rate is adopted rather than calibrated.

We fit the two-layer model in Eq.~\ref{eq:thermo_total} jointly to NOAA in situ thermosteric sea level estimates at 0--700\,m (1955--2025) and 0--2000\,m (2005--2025) \citep{Levitus2012}. We use Bayesian inference \citep{Tarantola2005} in which MCMC samples parameter combinations $\boldsymbol{\phi} = (a, b_u, b_d, \epsilon_u, \tau_u, \sigma_{\mathrm{extra}})$ with probability density proportional to the posterior (Fig.~\ref{fig:ocean_priors_posteriors}). Assuming the residual at each point is normally distributed about the model with variance $\sigma_i^2$, the negative log-likelihood takes the form $\frac{1}{2}\sum_i \left[(r_i(\boldsymbol{\phi})/\sigma_i)^2 + \ln\sigma_i^2\right]$. Adding a negative log-prior for $\tau_u$, whose log-normal prior (defined below) has mean $\mu_{\tau_u}$ and standard deviation $s_{\tau_u}$ in $\ln\tau_u$, gives the objective function
      \begin{equation}
      \mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\phi}) = \sum_{i} \left[\left(\frac{r_i(\boldsymbol{\phi})}{\sigma_i}\right)^2 + \ln\sigma_i^2\right] + \left(\frac{\ln\tau_u - \mu_{\tau_u}}{s_{\tau_u}}\right)^2,
      \label{eq:thermo_objective}
      \end{equation}
which is twice the negative log-posterior up to an additive constant. MCMC visits parameter combinations $\boldsymbol{\phi}$ with probability proportional to $\exp[-\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\phi})/2]$. Here, $r_i(\boldsymbol{\phi})$ is the residual between the demeaned model and demeaned observation at point $i$, and $\sigma_i^2$ is that point's total variance. The point variance is defined by the quadrature sum of the formal measurement error, a structural uncertainty retained after a bias correction in the NOAA thermosteric observations \citep{Li2022}, and $\sigma_{\mathrm{extra}}$, a noise term, shared across both the 0--700 m and 0--2000 m records, that absorbs misfit beyond the reported observational error. The MCMC sampling uses package emcee with 64 walkers, 6000 burn-in steps, and 30,000 production steps to ensure convergence. The relaxation time of the upper later, $\tau_u$, has an informative prior, $\log(\tau_u) \sim \mathcal{N}(\ln 8,0.5^2)$, or $\mu_{\tau_u} = \ln (8$ yr) and $s_{\tau_u} = 0.5$ yr, consistent with the range in \citet{Geoffroy2013}. The model parameters $b_u$ and $\tau_u$ are correlated in the likelihood---a slower upper-layer response can be partly offset by a larger sensitivity to fit the same trajectory---but this partial degeneracy is managed by the prior on $\tau_u$. Five parameters --- $a$, $b_u$, $b_d$, $\epsilon_u$, and $\log(\sigma_{\mathrm{extra}})$ --- get uninformative uniform priors with wide bounds (Fig. \ref{fig:ocean_priors_posteriors}). The posterior median and 90\% credible intervals are: $a = 20.0$ [10.1, 31.2] mm $^\circ$C$^{-2}$, $b_u = 51.0$ [40.6, 75.4] mm $^\circ$C$^{-1}$, $b_d = 68.6$ [37.0, 104.3] mm $^\circ$C$^{-1}$, $\epsilon_u = 0.023$ [$-0.112$, 0.140] mm yr$^{-1}$, $\tau_u = 11.6$ [6.2, 25.3] yr, and $\sigma_{\mathrm{extra}} = 2.0$ [1.7, 2.3] mm. The calibrated model reproduces both records with RMS residuals of 2.3 mm for depths 0--700 m and 1.3 mm for 0--2000 m, against 42 and 30 mm of observed rise over the respective records.

\subsubsection{Glaciers}

The rate of glacier mass loss has been shown to be approximately linear with warming \citep{Rounce2023, GlaMBIE2025}. In the interest of developing consistent uncertainty quantification for this study, we fit cumulative glacier sea-level contribution in level space as
\begin{equation}
	H_{\mathrm{gl}}(t) = b_{gl}\int_{t_0}^{t}Td\tau + c_{gl}(t - t_0) + H_{gl_0} + \varepsilon_{gl}(t),
	\label{eq:glacier_level}
\end{equation}
where $T$ is the GMST anomaly, evaluated at the annual resolution of GlaMBIE's own calendar-year-mean reporting (rather than on a finer monthly grid), $t_0$ is the study's baseline year (2000) at which $H_{gl_0}$ anchors the model's level, and $\varepsilon_{gl}(t)$ is jointly distributed as $\mathcal{N}(0,\, \Sigma_{gl})$, with $\Sigma_{gl}$ the true covariance of this cumulative (integrated) record, built directly from GlaMBIE's reported annual mass-balance uncertainties rather than treated as independent from point to point. We fit the model to the demeaned Glacier Mass Balance Intercomparison Exercise (GlaMBIE) record (2000--2023, 23 annual points) using Bayesian inference with weakly informative priors $b_{gl}\sim \mathcal{N}(0.61,\,2.0)$ mm yr$^{-1}$ $^\circ$C$^{-1}$, truncated at $b_{gl}\geq0$, and $c_{gl} \sim \mathcal{N}(0.3,1)$ mm yr$^{-1}$. The prior mean for $b_{gl}$ is drawn from \citet{Rounce2023}, whose reported end-of-century glacier contributions of 0.70 to 2.23 mm yr$^{-1}$ across warming scenarios of 1.5 to 4 $^\circ$C imply a sensitivity near 0.61 mm yr$^{-1}$ $^\circ$C$^{-1}$; its standard deviation is wide enough that the prior is only weakly informative, and the non-negativity truncation imposes the physical requirement that glaciers lose, rather than gain, mass as the climate warms. For the MCMC, we use package emcee with 32 walkers, 2000 burn-in steps, and 4000 production steps. The posterior median sensitivity is $b = 0.75$ mm yr$^{-1}$ $^\circ$C$^{-1}$ [0.30--1.20] and the committed rate is $c = 0.57$ mm yr$^{-1}$ [0.45--0.69], with a reduced chi-square of 1.04 against the reported GlaMBIE uncertainties confirming that this correlated error model is well specified (Fig. \ref{fig:component_fit_diagnostics}a, Fig. \ref{fig:glacier_corner}).

\subsubsection{Antarctic Peninsula}
\label{s:peninsula}

The Antarctic Peninsula component uses the same level-space Bayesian framework as glaciers, fit at annual resolution to the IMBIE-3 Peninsula record (1979--2023, 45 annual points; \citealp{Otosaka2026}), with the rate--temperature model $\dot{H}_{\mathrm{p}} = a_pT^2 + b_pT + c_p$. As with glaciers, $H_{p_0}$ is anchored at the record's own first point (1979) rather than at the start of the temperature record, and the true covariance of this cumulative record, built directly from IMBIE-3's reported annual mass-balance-rate uncertainties, is used directly in the likelihood via emcee (32 walkers, 2000 burn-in steps, and 4000 production steps). Unlike glaciers, we additionally fit a model-inadequacy term $\sigma_{p,\mathrm{extra}}$: the reduced chi-square without one is 2.64, indicating real annual scatter beyond IMBIE-3's reported uncertainty that a linear GMST-only model does not capture, whereas including it gives a reduced chi-square of 0.99. We fit both the linear ($a_p=0$) and quadratic model with priors $b_p\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\,2.0)$ mm yr$^{-1}$ $^\circ$C$^{-1}$ and $c_p \sim \mathcal{N}(0.3,1)$ mm yr$^{-1}$. Unlike glaciers, whose global-aggregate mass loss has no plausible mechanism for reversing with warming, we do not truncate $b_p$ at zero: the Peninsula's warming-driven surface melt and ice-shelf-collapse-triggered dynamic thinning \citep[e.g.,][]{Scambos2004} are well documented, but no Peninsula-specific literature result establishes a one-signed response strong enough to justify imposing it on the posterior, and a compensating accumulation response of the kind documented for East Antarctica (below) cannot be ruled out here. The Bayesian Information Criterion favors the linear model with posterior median sensitivity $b_p = 0.044$ mm yr$^{-1}$ $^\circ$C$^{-1}$ [$-0.020$, 0.108] and committed rate $c_p = 0.056$ mm\,yr$^{-1}$ [0.039, 0.072] (Fig. \ref{fig:component_fit_diagnostics}b); the interval spans zero, so this record does not, on its own, resolve the sign of the Peninsula's temperature sensitivity. The Peninsula contribution reflects combined SMB and glacier discharge responses to warming.


\subsubsection{East Antarctica}

The EAIS component follows the same corrected level-space framework as glaciers and the Peninsula, fit at annual resolution to the IMBIE-3 EAIS record (1979--2023, 45 annual points; \citep{Otosaka2026}), with $H_{e_0}$ anchored at the record's own first point and the true covariance of the cumulative record, built directly from IMBIE-3's reported annual mass-balance-rate uncertainties, used directly in the likelihood. The linear sensitivity $b_e$ is given a symmetric, literature-informed prior, $b_e \sim \mathcal{N}(-0.17,\,0.5)$ mm\,yr$^{-1}$\,$^\circ$C$^{-1}$, not truncated at zero: EAIS has no physical reason to favor a one-signed sensitivity, since warming-driven accumulation gain can plausibly outweigh any discharge response over this record. The prior mean is drawn from an independent literature surface-mass-balance sensitivity ($C_T = 60\pm20$ Gt\,yr$^{-1}$\,$^\circ$C$^{-1}$; \citealp{Frieler2015,Ligtenberg2013}), converted to this fit's sea-level-rise-positive sign convention. The posterior sensitivity is $b_e = -0.015$~mm\,yr$^{-1}$\,$^\circ$C$^{-1}$ [$-0.14$, 0.12] and committed rate $c_e = 0.007$~mm\,yr$^{-1}$ [$-0.03$, 0.04], with a reduced chi-square of 0.91 against the reported IMBIE-3 uncertainties (Fig.~\ref{fig:component_fit_diagnostics}c). This regression is a diagnostic check against IMBIE-3 observations; the EAIS forecast contribution is instead computed from the literature surface-mass-balance sensitivity described above.

\subsubsection{West Antarctica}

Unlike glaciers, the Antarctic Peninsula, and EAIS, we do not fit a rate--temperature regression to WAIS over the observational record. We instead fit a quadratic in time to the 1979--2023 IMBIE-3 mass balance time series \citep{Otosaka2026}. This fit uses the same Bayesian framework as \S\ref{s:peninsula} but replacing temperature with time. The acceleration $a$ is given a symmetric prior, $a \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\, \sigma=0.05\ \mathrm{mm\ yr^{-2}})$. The quadratic yields an acceleration of 0.012 mm yr$^{-2}$ [0.011--0.013] and a rate in year 2000 of 0.230 mm yr$^{-1}$ [0.215--0.246] (Fig.~\ref{fig:component_fit_diagnostics}d). We set the rate of WAIS loss to zero prior to 1979, when the IMBIE-3 time series begins and the fit to the data shows a rate $<0.05$ mm/yr.


\subsection{Forecasting methods and validation}
\label{s:forecasting_model}

The forecasts presented here use the IPCC AR6 GMST trajectories for different emission scenarios, though they can be applied to any GMST trajectory. We focus on the policy-relevant emission scenarios, which are labeled by their approximate median end-of-century global mean surface temperature anomaly (relative to preindustrial) rounded to the nearest integer: 2$\,^\circ$C (SSP1-2.6), 3$\,^\circ$C (SSP2-4.5), and 4$\,^\circ$C (SSP3-7.0). We initialize the GMSL forecast using the GMSL level, rate, and acceleration observed in the satellite-era and blend in the component-model sum in rate space, such that
\begin{equation}
	\dot{H}_{\mathrm{forecast}} = w\dot{H}_{\mathrm{obs}} + \bigl[1 - w\bigr]\dot{H}_{\mathrm{comp}},
	\label{eq:blend_rate}
\end{equation}
where $\dot{H}_{\mathrm{obs}}$ is the rate from the satellite-era quadratic fit of \citet{Hamlington2024,Nerem2022}, $\dot{H}_{\mathrm{comp}}$ is the rate of the sum of the calibrated components. The blending weight $w(t)$ is a sigmoid
\begin{equation}
	w = 1 - \Theta\left(\frac{t - t_r}{\tau_r}\right),
	\label{eq:sigmoid_weight}
\end{equation}
where $\Theta(x) =(1 + e^{-x})^{-1}$ is the standard logistic function, $t_r = 2035$, and $\tau_r = 5$ yr. At $t \ll t_r$, $w \approx 1$ and the forecast follows the observed quadratic trajectory. At $t \gg t_r$, $w \approx 0$ and the forecast follows the component-model physics. The transition spans approximately 22 yr, meaning that 10\%--90\% of the weight shift occurs over $\pm 2.2 \tau_r \approx \pm 11$ yr around $t_r$. We chose $t_r = 2035$ because it is more than a quarter and less than a half of the GMSL satellite altimetry record into the future, a period when the extrapolation of observations should have strong forecasting skill. The tapering recognizes the expected reduction in forecasting skill of the simple data extrapolation over timescales longer than roughly half of the observational window. Pushing $t_r$ to 5 years later (approximately half of the calibration window and arguably the latest viable choice) produces forecasts that are indistinguishable within uncertainty, making the results insensitive to the specific value of $t_r$ within the range of 10-15 years into the future.  

The GMSL forecast is obtained by integrating Eq.~\eqref{eq:blend_rate} forward from the observed level at the forecast origin ($t_0 = 2025$, the most recent NASA altimetry data as of this writing), so that 
\begin{equation}
	H_{\mathrm{forecast}}(t) = H_{\mathrm{obs}}(t_0) + \int_{t_0}^{t} \dot{H}_{\mathrm{forecast}}(t')\,dt'.
	\label{eq:blend_level}
\end{equation}
The blending is done sample-by-sample across the 2000-member Monte Carlo ensemble, preserving correlations between the quadratic-fit parameter uncertainty and the component-model uncertainty. Because the extrapolation is derived entirely from the satellite altimetry record, it carries no dependence on emissions pathway. The component sum, $\dot{H}_{\mathrm{comp}}$, is SSP-dependent through the temperature-forced component models. Consequently, the blended forecast is SSP-independent in the near term (where $w \approx 1$) and becomes progressively SSP-dependent as the component physics dominate at later times.

Uncertainty in $\dot{H}_{\mathrm{comp}}$ is propagated by Monte Carlo summation of the components. Each component has its own $2000$-member ensemble of level-space projections $H_k(t)$, drawn independently from that component's posterior or, for components using prescribed literature sensitivities rather than data calibration, from the corresponding parameter distributions. The total level is formed sample-by-sample, $H_{\mathrm{comp},i}(t)=\sum_k H_{k,i}(t)$ for $i=1,\dots,2000$, so draws from independently calibrated components are summed without pairing individual samples by any shared physical driver. The component rate $\dot{H}_{\mathrm{comp}}$ used in Eq.~\eqref{eq:blend_rate} is obtained by finite-differencing this summed ensemble in time before blending with the observational quadratic rate. Reported credible intervals (66\% and 90\%) are the 17th/83rd and 5th/95th empirical percentiles of the resulting ensemble at each time step.

\subsubsection{Component-level GMST dependence}

Not all of the SPECTRUM model components described in Section \ref{s:component_model_derivation} have direct, explicit dependences on GMST. These components include the Greenland glacier discharge, which depends on the temperature of the ocean surrounding Greenland, and the SMB of Greenland and EAIS. 

To project GrIS discharge model forward in time, we calibrate a transfer function between the Greenland regional surface temperature anomaly and the ocean temperature and then apply a normal distribution of Arctic amplification factors to calculate the Greenland regional surface temperature form projected GMST under different emission scenarios. The linear transfer function on annual-mean observations is
\begin{equation}
	T_{\mathrm{ocean}}(t) = \xi T_{\mathrm{surface}}^{\mathrm{Gr}}(t) + \beta + \varepsilon_{go}(t),
	\label{eq:ocean_transfer}
\end{equation}
where $T_{\mathrm{surface}}^{\mathrm{Gr}}$ is the Greenland regional surface temperature anomaly and $\varepsilon_{go}$ is a zero-mean residual. Both series are anomalies relative to the 1995--2005 baseline. An OLS fit over $52$ common annual values gives $\xi = 0.148 \pm 0.027$, $\beta = -0.051 \pm 0.031\,^\circ$C, and residual standard deviation $\sigma_{\varepsilon_{go}} = 0.20 ^\circ$C. 

For projections, SSP GMST trajectories are converted to Greenland regional surface temperature using an Arctic amplification factor $AA= 3.0 \pm 0.5$, consistent with the observed ratio of Greenland-to-global warming over 1970--2021. Greenland regional surface temperatures are the passed through Eq.~\eqref{eq:ocean_transfer} to obtain projected $T_{\mathrm{ocean}}$, which then enters Eq.~\eqref{eq:greenland_dyn} via the delayed integral. Each of the 2,000 Monte Carlo samples draws $\mathrm{AA} \sim \mathcal{N}(3.0, 0.5^2)$, $\xi \sim \mathcal{N}(0.148, 0.027^2)$, and $\beta \sim \mathcal{N}(-0.051, 0.031^2)$ jointly with $(\gamma, r_0, \delta)$ from the discharge posterior, so that Arctic amplification, transfer-function, and discharge parameter uncertainties all propagate into the projections. A single draw of $(\alpha, \beta, \gamma, r_0, \delta)$ is applied to all SSP scenarios, so that inter-SSP differences isolate the effect of the emissions pathway from parameter uncertainty.

For SMB in Greenland and EAIS, the forecast uses simple thermodynamic models calibrated from regional climate models. For Greenland SMB, the forecast uses a quadratic temperature scaling $\dot{H}_{\mathrm{smb}} = C_{T^2}\Delta T^2 + C_T \Delta T + \dot{M}_0$ with sensitivities drawn from the spread between regional climate models \citep{Fettweis2020, Noel2021, Glaude2024}. The linear sensitivity $C_T \sim \mathcal{N}(-300, 80^2)$~Gt yr$^{-1}$ $^\circ$C$^{-1}$ of GMST encompasses the MAR, RACMO, and HIRHAM estimates. The quadratic sensitivity, $C_{T^2} \sim \mathcal{N}(-50, 30^2)$~Gt yr$^{-1}$ $^\circ$C$^{-2}$, represents the effects of ablation zone expansion and melt-albedo feedback with warming. $\dot{M}_0 = 380$~Gt yr$^{-1}$ is the baseline SMB. For EAIS SMB, we use a linear model ($C_{T^2}=0$) and the literature-derived accumulation sensitivity $C_T = 60 \pm 20$~Gt yr$^{-1}$ $^\circ$C$^{-1}$ of GMST \citep{Frieler2015, Ligtenberg2013}, reflecting the Clausius--Clapeyron scaling of snowfall with warming ($C_T > 0$ corresponds to mass gain, i.e., negative SLR). Data from IMBIE show EAIS in approximate balance (Fig. \ref{fig:ocean_priors_posteriors}), so we set $\dot{M}_0 =0$. The EAIS contribution is small ($-13$ to $-30$~mm at 2100, depending on scenario) relative to the total, meaning the GMSL projection results of this study are largely insensitive to these parameter choices.

Terrestrial water storage projections are adopted directly from the IPCC AR6 rather than independently modeled, as the processes governing groundwater depletion, reservoir impoundment, and land hydrology changes are outside the scope of this framework and the data are insufficient to allow for a data-driven approach as with other components. At each decadal interval in the IPCC projections, we fit a Gaussian distribution to the reported median and inter-quantile spread and draw 2,000 Monte Carlo samples, which are then linearly interpolated to annual resolution. For the hindcast period (pre-2020), we use the \citet{Frederikse2020} TWS observations with Monte Carlo sampling from their reported uncertainties. TWS is SSP-dependent but the contribution is small (${\sim}30$~mm at 2100 under SSP2-4.5) and does not materially affect the total uncertainty budget.



\subsubsection{West Antarctic Ice Sheet} 

West Antarctica dominates the uncertainty in sea-level projections because its major outlet glaciers, particularly Thwaites and Pine Island, lie close to a threshold at which small changes in ice geometry could trigger self-sustaining retreat \citep{PattynMorlighem2020,Robel2019}. Whether that threshold has been, or will be, crossed depends on glacier-specific details that remain only partly observed. The underlying mechanism, known as the Marine Ice Sheet Instability (MISI), was first proposed several decades ago \citep{Mercer1968,Weertman1974} and later formalized in idealized settings \citep{Schoof2007}. In essence, MISI could arise where glaciers rest on beds that are below sea level and deepen inland, which allows thinning to float the ice off the seafloor, reducing basal drag, and allowing further acceleration and thinning. The assumptions that drive runaway in idealized models do not necessarily map to the present day West Antarctic Ice Sheet, where various processes complicate straightforward application of the concept of MISI. For example, ice shelves, the floating extensions of the ice sheet, buttress the glaciers upstream of them \citep{Gudmundsson2013,Pegler2018,HaseloffSergienko2018}. The magnitude of buttressing, and how it evolves as the glacier retreats, depends on the geometry of the bed and shelf, factors that vary across West Antarctica \citep{SergienkoWingham2019,SergienkoHindmarsh2013}. Buttressing can evolve on multi-annual timescales as evinced by direct observations of the Pine Island Ice Shelf show a near-total loss of buttressing stress over roughly the past decade, accompanying a 20\% increase in glacier velocity \citep{WellsMoran2026}, and ongoing evolution of the Thwaites ice shelf \citep{Alley2021}. Retreat of glaciers also can be driven independently by sustained ocean warming melting the ice from below \citep{Jenkins2018,Davis2023}, a process nominally distinct from a genuinely self-sustaining runaway (MISI) in that it depends on continued oceanic heat delivery rather than on geometry and buoyancy alone \citep{Joughin2014,Favier2014}. In practice, however, the distinction between an internally driven runaway retreat and one driven by continued oceanic heating is not clear cut because the two can produce near-identical retreat over the coming decades while implying markedly different outcomes over subsequent centuries \citep{Sergienko2026,Hill2023,Reese2023}. Recent regional ocean modeling finds that such heat delivery is itself largely committed over the coming decades, regardless of near-term emissions pathways \citep{Naughten2023}, and current melt rates may already be sufficient to have triggered self-sustaining retreat independently of any further increase in forcing \citep{ArthernWilliams2017}. This combination of a physically plausible runaway mechanism, glacier-specific buttressing whose durability is difficult to assess, and the challenge of distinguishing reversible from irreversible retreat using present-day data, together make West Antarctica's future contribution to GMSL the largest and most consequential source of uncertainty in current projections \citep{Bamber2019}. As a result, current projections of West Antarctica's GMSL contribution span a wide range, from several centimeters to several tens of centimeters by 2100 under high-emissions scenarios, according to the multi-model assessments underlying IPCC AR6 \citep{Fox-Kemper2021,Seroussi2020,Edwards2021}. 

%This range arises less from disagreement over future warming and more from uncertainties in the physics of the ice sheet. Ice-sheet models differ substantially in their treatment of buttressing, basal melting, and grounding-line dynamics, such that forcing different models with the same climate scenario yields markedly different outcomes even before observational uncertainty is considered \citep{Payne2021,Seroussi2020}. This structural disagreement between models, rather than uncertainty in the forcing, is now understood to be the dominant source of spread \citep{Edwards2021}, and yet is incomplete, having not been fully explored due to the absence of systematic sensitivity studies in ice sheet models. One example of an unexplored source of uncertainty arising from how models represent the dynamics of glaciers is the stress exponent $n$ governing the nonlinear viscosity of ice in the flow law. Nearly all ice-sheet models assume $n = 3$, although laboratory experiments and observations suggests that $n = 4$ may better represent ice flow in relevant areas \citep{Millstein2022,Ranganathan2024}. This single parameter choice markedly accelerates projected loss from the Amundsen Sea Embayment and produces sea-level-rise rates that are highly sensitive to its value across a range of model benchmarks \citep{GetraerMorlighem2025,Martin2026}. Furthermore, a small number of models incorporate more speculative mechanisms, such as the rapid structural failure of tall ice cliffs following the loss of their supporting ice shelf. This process, broadly known as the Marine Ice Cliff Instability (MICI), can raise projected contributions substantially, toward a meter or more by 2100 \citep{DeContoP2016,DeConto2021}. However, the physical basis and likelihood of this mechanism remain actively debated \citep{Clerc2019,Morlighem2024,SunGudmundsson2026}, and most standard projections either exclude MICI or treat it as a low-probability tail. Model divergence is compounded by model initialization itself, which currently relies on initializing to snapshots or model spin-ups \citep{Seroussi2020,Fricker2025}. 

Given these complexities and uncertainties, we do not attempt to constrain a GMST sensitivity for WAIS. Such an effort is beyond the scope of this work. Instead, we develop a two-scenario mixture model to represent a range of potential GMSL contributions from WAIS in the 21st century. Based on growing evidence that WAIS mass loss is primarily sensitive to internal ice dynamics and deep ocean temperatures that are likely to change slowly in the coming decades, we simplify our WAIS projections by making them independent of the warming trajectory \citep{Naughten2023,Coulon2024,Rosier2025}, meaning the range of 21st-century outcomes is controlled by ice dynamics that are insensitive to emissions over our forecasting horizon in the SPECTRUM model.  

In Scenario 1 (S1), mass loss continues along the observed trajectory (Fig. \ref{fig:component_fit_diagnostics}d). Extrapolating the trend gives a median GMSL contribution of 84 mm by 2100, closely matching the median of the ISMIP6 emulator, 85 mm \citep{Edwards2021}, the only GMSL projection source with a symmetric distribution included in AR6. The median also falls between the modes of two skewed external assessments, LARMIP-2 \citep{Levermann2020}, with mode 73 mm, median 137 mm, and skewness 2.42, and the structured expert judgment \citep{Bamber2019}, with mode 120 mm, median 207 mm, and skewness 2.40. A 45-year observational record cannot itself constrain how much additional error should accumulate from extrapolating a further 76 years beyond it, so a confidence interval built from the fitted parameters alone would understate the true uncertainty in this extrapolation. We instead treat the reported growth in uncertainty beyond the last observation in the ISMIP6 emulator as an external estimate of the extrapolation error, applying it as a prediction error following \citet{Tarantola2005}, and add it to our parameter uncertainty. This widens our 90\% CI at 2100 to [$-13$, 180] mm, closely matching the range of the ISMIP6 emulator of [$-11$, 182] mm. The S1 probability distribution, exceedance curve, and time-series are shown in Fig \ref{fig:wais_p_s1_sweep} for the case where the probability of S1, p(S1), is 100\%.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Validation} 
\subsubsection{Total GMSL budget closure}
\label{s:budget_close}

Satellite altimetry data collected from 1993--2025 provide observations of GMSL \citep{Beckley2017,Hamlington2020} that can be compared with the sum of the independently calibrated model components (Fig. \ref{fig:budget_closure}). Total GMSL is withheld from calibration to serve as a validation data set. For Greenland SMB, which we do not calibrate due to data sparseness, we use RACMO-derived SMB \citep{Mouginot2019}. Terrestrial water storage is adopted from \citet{Frederikse2020}. 

The component sum closes 97--106\% of the observed GMSLR rate over three epochs (1993--2025, 2000--2025, 2010--2025), offering validation that the individually calibrated components together approximate GMSL during the instrumental record. Because each component is calibrated independently, we treat their rate uncertainties as independent and combine them in quadrature. Each component's individual uncertainty comes directly from its Monte Carlo ensemble. For a given epoch, we fit a linear trend to every ensemble member and take the standard deviation of the resulting trends. Repeating this for each epoch lets the combined uncertainty differ from one epoch to the next, rather than using one fixed value throughout the satellite record. By this measure, the residual is within roughly one combined standard deviation of the component rate uncertainties in the two longer epochs and reaches 1.7$\sigma$ in the shortest (2000--2025) epoch (0.70$\sigma$, 1.70$\sigma$, and 0.70$\sigma$ for 1993--2025, 2000--2025, and 2010--2025, respectively).

\subsubsection{Hindcasting the level, rate, and transient sensitivity}

We validate the combined GMST-sensitive model by two hindcasting approaches. Both are generated by forcing the model components with observed GMST back to 1900 \citep{Rohde2020}, with an emphasis on post-1950 when increasingly reliable GMSL data are available. The first comparison is between global mean sea level and rate produced by SPECTRUM with data from \citet{Fox-Kemper2021} and \citet{Frederikse2020}. For the second comparison, we calculate the transient sensitivity of the SPECTRUM output and GMSL data, where the transient sensitivity is defined as $\alpha = d\dot{S}/dT$ with $\dot{S}$ as the rate of change in GMSL. Here, we use the same $t_0$ starting point (2025, the end of the satellite altimetry record) and tapering scheme as described in the forecasting section (\S\ref{s:forecasting_model}) to ensure the hindcast differs only from the forecast in the GMST trend used to drive the model. %While a hindcast analysis should be seen as a necessary condition for presenting a forecast, the sensitivity test is arguably the more important validation because a sea-level projection model is simply a way of representing the sensitivity of GMSL to warming to allow for calculations of GMSL given a GMST forcing.

Extending the hindcast back to 1900 shows two distinct regimes relative to the GMSL reconstructed time-series (Fig. \ref{fig:observations}a). From approximately 1950 to the present, the hindcast agrees closely with the \citet{Frederikse2020} and \citep{Fox-Kemper2021} GMSL reconstructions and with the satellite altimetry record \citep{Beckley2017}. The hindcast rate of GMSL rise over 1950--2000 (1.11 mm yr$^{-1}$) matches both reconstructions (1.02 and 1.01 mm yr$^{-1}$), and the hindcast level at 1950 is within 10 mm of each. Before 1950, the hindcast diverges from the \citet{Frederikse2020} reconstruction, understating the reconstructed rate of rise (0.20 mm yr$^{-1}$ versus 1.39--1.64 mm yr$^{-1}$ over 1900--1950) and level (roughly 70--75 mm low by 1900, relative to the 2000 baseline). This divergence coincides with the period in which the reconstructions rely increasingly on sparse tide-gauge coverage and model-based infilling and our model suffers from a lack of constraints on mass changes in the Greenland Ice Sheet and glaciers. The limitations of our model arise by design: we chose to focus only on model representations that can be fit to observations and that represent the ongoing response to warming. This choice excludes more complete and complex land-ice process representations that would be necessary to represent GMSL during colder periods in the past, as this is not our goal. We therefore treat the pre-1950 hindcast as qualitatively informative but do not draw quantitative conclusions from it. We restrict our validation claims to the 75-year, post-1950 period, where component calibration data are available and the agreement with observations is close.

The transient sensitivity of our component model, which defines the change in the rate of change in GMSL with warming, tracks closely with the observed sensitivities (Fig. \ref{fig:observations}b). It is worth emphasizing that this agreement include the transition from the earlier period when thermosteric sea-level rise of the largest contributor to GMSL to the present when the sum of land-ice and terrestrial water storage (barystatic) are larger overall contributors to GMSK. The transient sensitivity of the observations and model also agree with the semi-empirical results of \citet{Rahmstorf2007} (Fig. \ref{fig:observations}b), which continue to track observations nearly 20 years after publication, showing that the simple semi-empirical model continues to provide a reasonable first-estimate of GMSL rise with warming. 

Together, the level, rate, and transient-sensitivity comparisons indicate that our framework reproduces the observed behavior of GMSLR over a 75-year window (1950--2025) spanning roughly 1 $^\circ$C of GMST change, without having been calibrated on observations of GMSL itself. This window includes three forms of agreement: the hindcast level tracks observations to within 10 mm, the hindcast rate matches observed decadal trends to within about 10\%, and the hindcast transient sensitivity reproduces both the observed rate--temperature curvature and its underlying physical cause (the shift from thermosteric to barystatic dominance). To our knowledge, no other GMSL forecasting framework or model presents this level of detailed validation nor hindcast skill. Because this validation window is comparable in duration and magnitude of warming to the near- and mid-term forecast horizon, it supports extending the same confidence forward to a similar span, roughly a century and a further 1--2 $^\circ$C of warming, consistent with the 75-year window and 10-year taper used to blend observed and component-model trends in the forecast itself (\S\ref{s:forecasting_model}). This validated envelope does not, however, extend to processes for which comparable historical constraints do not exist, which is why WAIS, the largest source of forecast uncertainty, is treated separately through a scenario-based `storyline', rather than data-driven approach, in this work.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Orthogonal dimensions of sea-level risk}

The first risk dimension is sensitive to emissions reduction. The temperature-dependent components are sensitive to changes in global mean surface temperature. In this work, we calibrate the sensitivities using decades of observations. Their collective contribution shifts systematically across warming trajectories and is reducible by emissions cuts (Fig. \ref{fig:components_voi}a). Under the `slow WAIS' scenario, the forecast narrows to a 90\% range of only $\sim$14 cm, an order of magnitude narrower than the full scenario cast that allows for rapid mass loss from WAIS due to internal feedbacks (Fig.~\ref{fig:ridge_projections}). In the slow scenario, emissions policy is the primary lever for managing risk: the difference between 2$\,^\circ$C and 4$\,^\circ$C warming is roughly 40 cm of additional rise, which roughly translates to 30 million additional people exposed to coastal flood risks and US\$50B/yr in coastal adaptation costs.

The second risk dimension is relatively insensitive to emissions reductions. WAIS dynamics accounts for virtually all of the total forecast variance and is largely decoupled from the emission pathway. The tail of the forecast distribution---the outcomes that drive the most severe human and economic consequences---is controlled almost entirely by whether WAIS retreat remains gradual at modern rates of loss or transitions to rapid, irreversible retreat. Emissions reduction decreases the median but does not substantively change the tail of the distribution. The probability of exceeding 2 m relative to pre-industrial is 3\% under the 2$\,^\circ$C trajectory and 7\% under 4$\,^\circ$C, with a 5\% probability across emission scenarios. The 3\% lower bound on this range highlights the fact that emission reductions alone cannot retire the high risk outcomes.

The risks and costs to coastal communities helps to highlight the dichotomy between the two possible scenarios of fast vs slow WAIS mass loss, or equivalently, emissions-insensitive vs emissions sensitive (Figs. \ref{fig:ridge_projections} and \ref{fig:components_voi}a). The difference at the 5\% exceedance probability, one possible design threshold for coastal adaptation that we denote by stars in Fig. \ref{fig:ridge_projections}b, is 1.0 m of sea-level rise by 2100 under the current policy warming trajectory (3 $^\circ$C). This difference translates into roughly 186M more people exposed to annual flood risks, US\$134B/yr in coastal adaptation, and US\$13.3T/yr ($\sim$11\% global GDP per year) in coastal flood damages without further adaptation. These large numbers reflect global population densities, with the highest densities near coastlines, and the fact that the locations of coastal communities and infrastructure are calibrated to lower sea levels. For intuition, the fast-WAIS scenario at 5\% exceedance implies an order of magnitude more sea level rise in the next 75 years than was experienced in the past 125 years, while the slow-WAIS scenario implies a factor of 3.5 more rise.

Resolving the question of WAIS mass loss rates is among the most financially consequential goals in modern science (Fig.~\ref{fig:components_voi}c). A value-of-information analysis shows that perfect knowledge of whether WAIS will lose mass slowly or rapidly is worth of order US\$5T in avoided costs, with the value peaking when confidence in a slow-WAIS future reaches 80\% and the information arrives early enough to inform infrastructure decisions. The value decays with time because late-arriving information leaves fewer years of decisions to improve, and because all forecasts, including this study, degrade as they extend beyond their calibration bounds. These numbers frame the research investment question in concrete terms: even partial reduction of WAIS uncertainty, well short of perfect knowledge, would justify annual research expenditures orders of magnitude above current levels. The return on that investment is determined by how quickly the observations can narrow the range of WAIS outcomes, which depends on both the rate of data collection and the development of reliable process models, like those used in the IPCC, capable of providing explanatory insights that underscore deep understanding of ice sheets and climate. 

Our forecast identifies a single measurement that would do more to reduce sea-level uncertainty than any other: the rate of West Antarctic ice loss over the next two decades. If WAIS mass loss continues near current rates through the 2030s and 2040s, that would build the case for the slow-WAIS scenario and allow the forecast to narrow toward the predictable, lower, emissions-sensitive range where adaptation costs are more manageable. If instead grounding-line retreat accelerates beyond the pace of our central scenario, the distribution would shift toward higher outcomes and 1.5--2 m of rise would move from a tail risk to a planning baseline. Either outcome would reshape coastal adaptation worldwide and would justify investment well beyond current levels. The observations needed to distinguish these futures can be made within a generation, but only if the observing systems and research programs are sustained and expanded at a scale that reflects the costs at stake.

Scientific research is a necessary condition for providing actionable information for coastal adaptation, but it is as likely as not that further research will add to the growing evidence to support rapid WAIS mass loss rather than build confidence in a slow-WAIS future \citep{Fricker2025}. Sustaining a wide range of uncertainties that admit $<1$ m and $> 2$ m of GMSLR in 2100 at 10\% probabilities, will force decisions on coastal planning, adaptation, and managed retreat that impact millions of lives and cost billions of dollars to be made under large uncertainties. The extent of these uncertainties in terms of lives, property, and ecosystems raises the question: Can we take action to increase the potential stability of West Antarctica? How much harm to people and the environment can we prevent? Recently, some research directions have been proposed to answer this question \citep{MacAyeal2024} with initial models of glacier interventions aimed at stabilizing WAIS show promise \citep{Meyer2026}. As with any nascent research, there are many unresolved questions about the feasibility, unintended consequences, and governance of these interventions \citep{Siegert2025}. There are risks in intervening to remediate the West Antarctic Ice Sheet and slow its loss and the logistical challenges will undoubtedly be great, but these risks and challenges must be weighed against the risks and challenges of sea-level rise as described in this study. There is no zero risk, zero cost response to sea level rise. The costs and challenges are real and must be borne regardless of what decisions are made. The questions are how much cost, how much challenge, and how much harm. The answers hinge on how fast West Antarctica will lose ice, and nothing short of a virtual guarantee in slow WAIS mass loss provides comfort for the world's coastlines.

We consider robust, well-calibrated process models to be high-value targets for research and development, but the evidence suggests these models are not ready to be used for planning that costs billions of dollars and impacts millions of lives.

A specific example is that modeling efforts for ISMIP6, which informed IPCC AR6 projections, were largely unfunded, leading to a dearth of model runs and near-absence of model parameter-sensitivity studies \citep{Aschwanden2021}. The world is going to endure high costs from sea-level rise (Fig. \ref{fig:ridge_projections}b). The questions are: how high will those costs be, and will we invest now to understand where and how to target funding to reduce the harm to people, ecosystems, and infrastructure, or continue on our current path? Our analysis shows that this investment must be directed along two independent axes---reducing emissions to compress the predictable components, and understanding and potentially stabilizing the West Antarctic Ice Sheet to address the dominant, emission-insensitive uncertainty that defines the upper bound of 21st-century sea-level rise.
