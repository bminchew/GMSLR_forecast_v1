\title{A Data-Driven, Component-Level Projection Framework for 21st-Century Sea-Level Rise}
\author{Minchew, Meyer, et al.}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
As the rise in global mean sea level (GMSL) accelerates and warming carries projections beyond the conditions their models were calibrated on, the central challenge is validating those models against withheld observations spanning a comparable change in warming. We present a framework for projecting GMSL that uses a simple physical model of each contributor, and we withhold observed GMSL from every calibration to test the sum of the components. Thermal expansion, glaciers, the Antarctic Peninsula, and Greenland ice discharge are calibrated by Bayesian inference against independent observations, with discharge modeled as a delayed response to ocean warming. Greenland surface mass balance is emulated, and East Antarctic snowfall and terrestrial water storage are adopted from published work. The component sum accounts for 89--104\% of the satellite-altimetry rate since 1993. Forced with observed warming over 1950--2025 (1.0--1.1 $^\circ$C), the components match satellite-era rates. Future West Antarctic Ice Sheet (WAIS) acceleration is poorly constrained by observations and models, so we use two storylines adapted from the literature. Under the slow-WAIS storyline, which assigns 90\% probability to continued loss along the observed trajectory, median GMSL rise over 2000--2100 is 0.45, 0.54, and 0.64 m for scenarios leading to 1.8, 2.7, and 3.6 $^\circ$C above pre-industrial. Under the fast-WAIS storyline, which assigns a 90\% probability to accelerated loss, the medians are 0.60, 0.69, and 0.79 m. The fast-WAIS 95th percentile lies about 1 m above the median in all three emission scenarios.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Sea-level rise will continue for centuries even if warming stops, so the question for coastal communities is not whether to adapt but how fast \citep{Fox-Kemper2021}. About 250 million people live on land below the present annual flood level, and the sea-level rise projected by 2100 under the Representative Concentration Pathway 4.5 (RCP4.5) emissions scenario would raise that number to about 360 million, assuming the 2010 population and no coastal defenses \citep{Kulp2019}. Without further adaptation, global flood damages could reach US\$14 trillion per year at 0.86 m of rise \citep{Jevrejeva2018}. The Sixth Assessment Report of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (AR6) projects median rises of 0.44 and 0.56 m by 2100, relative to 1995--2014, for low- and intermediate-emissions scenarios (the Shared Socioeconomic Pathways SSP1-2.6 and SSP2-4.5) \citep{Fox-Kemper2021}. AR6 also attributes deep uncertainty to ice-sheet processes that models do not represent well \citep{Fox-Kemper2021}. High-end estimates published since AR6 span 0.6--2.0 m of rise by 2100 \citep{Garner2026}, and coastal planners use widely varying projections \citep{Hirschfeld2023}.

Whatever method is used, continued warming ensures that these projections will extend beyond the conditions in the observational record. The central difficulty is therefore not building a model but establishing when its projections can be trusted. A model earns credibility when it is calibrated with observations and tested against observations withheld from calibration. A test against withheld data is most informative when the withheld record spans a change in forcing comparable to the change being forecast. Such a test does not remove the out-of-sample problem, but it estimates how far a model can be carried beyond the conditions used for calibration. Projections can also be tested against observations made after they were published. The mid-range projection of the IPCC Second Assessment Report matched global sea-level rise over the following three decades to within about 10 mm, while its projections of individual contributors were more variable \citep{Tornqvist2025}.

A model must also report its uncertainties in a form that separates their sources \citep{Aschwanden2021}. Uncertainty from future emissions can be reduced by policy, whereas uncertainty from poorly understood ice-sheet processes can be reduced only by new observations and better models. A recent community synthesis makes this case, calling for projections narrowed by observations and for uncertainty characterized by its structure and not only by its magnitude \citep{Felikson2025}.

Sea-level projection models span a wide range of complexity. They meet these requirements to different degrees, but none meets them fully. At the most detailed end are process-based ice-sheet models, which solve the equations of ice flow and deformation and can test competing hypotheses about the mechanics of ice loss \citep{Schoof2007,Morlighem2024}. They are needed to project ice loss under conditions outside any observational record. Reproducing observed mass change is a necessary, though not sufficient, condition for trusting their projections \citep{Aschwanden2021}, and this condition is hard to meet. One obstacle is that their parameters are inferred by solving inverse problems in which the data constrain only a few combinations of parameters \citep{Petra2014,Isaac2015}. As of 2020, no ice-sheet model run forward in time had reproduced the sixfold increase in ice-sheet mass imbalance observed since the early 1990s \citep{Siegert2020}. Many Antarctic projections published in recent years show the ice sheet gaining mass over the first few years of the simulations, while observations over the same years show it losing mass \citep{Fricker2025,Seroussi2024}.

Projections also depend on how each model is initialized and calibrated \citep{Seroussi2020,Goldberg2026}. Some models are run to equilibrium with past climate before the projection begins. They change little on their own but miss the observed present-day mass loss. Others are initialized to match the present geometry and flow, and these may not reproduce the observed rate of mass change \citep{PattynMorlighem2020,vandenAkker2025}. In the Antarctic ensemble of the Ice Sheet Model Intercomparison Project (ISMIP6), the choice of ice-sheet model accounts for 52\% of the uncertainty in projected dynamic mass loss by 2100, compared with 13\% for the choice of climate model \citep{Seroussi2023}. The projected Antarctic contribution to 2100 shows no clear response to the emissions scenario \citep{Edwards2021}. These models also lack a validated representation of iceberg calving and fracture \citep{PattynMorlighem2020,Bassis2024,Benn2026,Jordan2026}. Calving is a leading-order control on ice-sheet mass loss. It accounts for about two thirds of the ice lost from Greenland and about half of that lost from Antarctica \citep{Jordan2026}, and calving can remove the buttressing that resists the seaward flow of the grounded ice. Since 2007, calving has reduced the buttressing of Antarctic ice shelves as much as ice-shelf thinning has \citep{Greene2022}. This and other shortcomings in the fidelity of ice sheet models motivated AR6 to complement these models with structured expert judgment in its low-confidence projections \citep{Bamber2019,Fox-Kemper2021}.

At the other extreme are approaches anchored directly in the observed record of global mean sea level (GMSL). The simplest extrapolates the observed rate and acceleration of GMSL rise forward in time \citep{Nerem2022}. Semi-empirical models fit the rate of GMSL rise to global mean surface temperature (GMST) \citep{Rahmstorf2007,Vermeer2009,Rahmstorf2012,Kopp2016}. Both are transparent and fit the quantity being predicted directly. However, they give no insight into which processes drive the rise, and the fitted relationship need not hold when contributors with different response times change their relative importance \citep{Church2013}.

Between these extremes sit component-level models, which constrain each physical contributor separately, trading spatial detail for parsimony and transparency \citep{Perrette2013,Kopp2014,Mengel2016,Nauels2017,Wong2017,Kopp2023}. Component-level frameworks differ in how each contributor is constrained and in whether the observed GMSL record is used in calibration or kept for testing. When every calibrated component is fitted to its own independent record and GMSL is withheld throughout, as in \citet{Mengel2016}, the sum of the calibrated components can be tested against observed GMSL.

No single class of model can simultaneously maximize physical detail, transparency, and observational constraint. Ordered by complexity, models can instead form a hierarchy in which each level is calibrated and tested against data before the next level of complexity is trusted. Ordering models from idealized to comprehensive is long established in climate science, where it has helped close the gap between simulation and understanding \citep{Held2005,Jeevanjee2017,Maher2019}. Such a hierarchy does not require any one model to be correct. It requires only that its models rest on assumptions different enough that their agreement or disagreement is informative. Where structurally different models agree, a result rests on more than one set of assumptions \citep{Lipscomb2025}. Where they disagree, the divergence shows what the data leave unconstrained and which observations would constrain it.

Simpler levels also bring practical advantages. Across 97 published comparisons, simple forecasting methods were as accurate as or more accurate than complex ones in 84\% of cases \citep{Green2015}. In a competition on 100,000 time series, about two-thirds of the submitted methods did not outperform a simple combination of exponential-smoothing forecasts \citep{Makridakis2020}. The assumptions of simple models are also easier to state and check, which helps those who use the results find errors and omissions \citep{Green2015}. A hierarchy built this way complements process-based ice-sheet models and aids their development rather than substituting for them.

Here we construct a data-driven, component-level forecast of 21st-century sea-level rise. We call the framework SPECTRUM, for Sea-level Projections from Empirical Calibration of Thermodynamical, Reduced-physics Models. Each contributor that the observational record can constrain is represented by a parsimonious physical model and calibrated by Bayesian inference against its own independent record. Among these, Greenland ice discharge is modeled as a lagged response to the temperature of the surrounding ocean and calibrated with observed discharge. Observed GMSL is withheld from every calibration. The summed components can therefore be tested against observations from 1950 to 2025, a period spanning about 1.0--1.1 $^\circ$C of warming, comparable to the warming that remains this century under intermediate emissions. We place SPECTRUM in a hierarchy and compare its forecast with extrapolation of the observed trend, a semi-empirical model, and the AR6 assessment. Where the record cannot constrain a contributor, as for the West Antarctic Ice Sheet (WAIS), we represent the range of outcomes with storylines rather than a calibrated model, keeping this uncertainty separate from the others. This separation lets us ask which part of 21st-century sea-level rise can be reduced by cutting emissions. It also shows which part of the uncertainty can be narrowed only by observations and models that constrain how fast West Antarctica will lose ice.



\section{A data-driven, component-level projection framework}
%% ================================================================

SPECTRUM represents each contributor to GMSL with its own model. How each model is constrained depends on the available data (Fig.~\ref{fig:spectrum_schematic}). Thermal expansion, glaciers, the Antarctic Peninsula, and Greenland ice discharge are calibrated by Bayesian inference against independent multi-decade observational records, listed in Table~S1 of the Supplementary Material. Greenland surface mass balance is emulated from regional climate model simulations. East Antarctic surface mass balance and terrestrial water storage are adopted from published sensitivities and assessments. West Antarctica is represented by slow-WAIS and fast-WAIS storylines. Each temperature-sensitive component is forced by GMST, directly or through a regional temperature derived from it. Its calibrated or adopted temperature dependence defines its forecast. Observed GMSL is withheld from the calibrations and used to test the sum of the components.

Earlier component-level frameworks constrain their contributors in different ways. Several draw their components from process models, assessments, or expert judgment. \citet{Kopp2014} build the distribution of each contributor from process-model ensembles, the IPCC Fifth Assessment Report (AR5), and expert elicitation. The MAGICC sea-level model \citep{Nauels2017} emulates process-based projections, with each component calibrated with output from climate, glacier, or ice-sheet models. FACTS, the Framework for Assessing Changes To Sea-level \citep{Kopp2023}, draws its land-ice contributions from emulators of model intercomparison projects or from structured expert judgment.

Others use observed GMSL in calibration. BRICK, the Building blocks for Relevant Ice and Climate Knowledge model \citep{Wong2017}, calibrates its component models against observations and then conditions the assembled ensemble on observed GMSL. In the high ice-sheet case of \citet{Perrette2013}, the ice-sheet contribution is the residual of a semi-empirical model calibrated with observed GMSL.

The design closest to SPECTRUM is that of \citet{Mengel2016}, who calibrate a semi-empirical relation for each contributor against its own observations, using estimates of long-term commitment as constraints, and compare the sum with total sea-level reconstructions not used in the calibration. SPECTRUM follows that design and differs from it in three ways. SPECTRUM parameters are estimated by Bayesian inference, which yields their joint posterior distribution, rather than by least squares. Greenland ice discharge is forced by the temperature of the ocean around Greenland, whereas \citet{Mengel2016} drive it with GMST. Finally, \citet{Mengel2016} note that their method cannot represent processes that proceed independently of the forcing, such as a collapse of the Amundsen Sea sector of West Antarctica. SPECTRUM represents the range of West Antarctic outcomes with storylines instead.

Unless otherwise noted, all sea-level values in the main text are global-mean 21st-century contributions referenced to the year 2000, given as the median with the 90\% credible interval in brackets. Throughout, we label each emissions scenario by the AR6 central estimate of its global mean surface temperature over 2081--2100 relative to the 1850--1900 (pre-industrial) mean \citep[][Table~4.5]{Lee2021}: 1.4 $^\circ$C (SSP1-1.9), 1.8 $^\circ$C (SSP1-2.6), 2.7 $^\circ$C (SSP2-4.5), 3.6 $^\circ$C (SSP3-7.0), and 4.4 $^\circ$C (SSP5-8.5). The central paths of the 1.8, 2.7, and 3.6 $^\circ$C scenarios reach 1.7, 2.7, and 3.9 $^\circ$C in 2099, the last year of the AR6 projections. We hold each path at its 2099 value thereafter.

\subsection{Component models, calibration, and validation}
\label{s:component_model_derivation}

\subsubsection{Greenland Ice Sheet}

The Greenland Ice Sheet (GrIS) is among the largest land-ice contributors to sea-level rise and has lost mass every year since 1998 \citep{Mouginot2019}. That loss is the imbalance between solid-ice discharge and surface mass balance (SMB), where SMB is the difference between mass gained by snow accumulation and mass lost to meltwater runoff and sublimation. GrIS discharge is sensitive to ocean temperature and surface melt runoff \citep{Straneo2013,Slater2020,Wood2021}. Warm subsurface waters thin and undercut the calving fronts of marine-terminating glaciers, driving increased calving and net terminus retreat. This effect is amplified where surface runoff drives mixing and buoyant upwelling along the calving face \citep{Slater2022}. Surface melt also lubricates glacier beds, but that effect is generally short-lived and localized as the subglacial hydrological system evolves to accommodate changes in meltwater flux \citep{Schoof2010,Tedstone2013,Ultee2022}. We therefore model discharge as a lagged response to ocean temperature.

\subsubsubsection{Discharge}
We approximate the GrIS-wide discharge as
\begin{equation}
	D = \gamma\,T_{\mathrm{ocean}}(t - \delta) + r_0,
	\label{eq:greenland_rate}
\end{equation}
where $D$ is the GrIS discharge in sea-level equivalent (SLE) units, $\gamma$ is an effective discharge sensitivity to ocean temperature, $\delta$ is the lag between warming of the ocean around Greenland and the response of discharge, $r_0$ is the background discharge rate at the baseline ocean temperature, and $T_{\mathrm{ocean}}$ is the ocean temperature anomaly in the waters around Greenland relative to the 1995--2005 mean. SLE is the rise in global mean sea level that would result if a given ice mass were added to the ocean, with 1~mm\,SLE${}= 362.5$~Gt throughout this study. Because $D$ is the gross discharge flux rather than an anomaly, it becomes a contribution to GMSL only when combined with SMB. We emulate SMB separately from a regional climate model (Section~\ref{s:greenland_smb}).

We calibrate against total GrIS discharge, summed over the 219 glaciers classed as marine-terminating in the \citet{Mouginot2019} dataset, of which 107 glaciers (85\% of discharge) are measured directly. Summing sacrifices spatial detail but reduces noise relative to the signal. Our goal is to capture ice-sheet-scale discharge rather than that of individual basins and glaciers. We calibrate the time-integrated form of Eq.~\ref{eq:greenland_rate}, which gives the cumulative discharge,
\begin{equation}
	H_{\mathrm{D}}(t) = \int_{t_0}^{t} D(t')\,\mathrm{d}t',
	\label{eq:greenland_dyn}
\end{equation}
with $\gamma$ and $r_0$ held constant in time. Fitting the cumulative record reduces the influence of single-year noise and directly constrains the total mass lost, the quantity of interest in this study. The errors of the cumulative record are correlated in time, which we account for below.

The calibration uses the ice discharge time series of \citet{Mouginot2019}, which spans the 47 years from 1972 to 2018, and ocean temperatures from EN4 (v4.2.2) \citep{Good2013}. We average EN4 over 200--500~m depth, the range most relevant to the grounding lines of Greenland's marine-terminating glaciers, where the glaciers lose contact with their beds \citep{Slater2020}, and over the seas surrounding Greenland south of 80$^\circ$N (58--80$^\circ$N, 75--5$^\circ$W). This geographic limit is set by the sparse subsurface sampling farther north.

Because the data are resolved annually, the lag $\delta$ takes integer-year values. For each candidate lag from 4 to 8~yr, we fit $(\gamma, r_0)$ by weighted least squares to the annual increments of the cumulative discharge, which are the yearly discharge values, each weighted by its reported uncertainty (Fig.~\ref{fig:greenland_delay_fit}). The cumulative record is a running sum of these yearly values, whose errors are independent, so this fit accounts for the correlation of errors in the cumulative record. We use EN4 from 1970 onward, so the delayed forcing is undefined in the first years of the discharge record, and every candidate lag is fitted to the same 41 years, 1978 to 2018, the years that the delayed forcing covers at the longest lag. The residuals scatter more widely than the reported uncertainties allow, with reduced chi-square $\chi^2/\mathrm{dof} = 1.45$ on 38 degrees of freedom at the preferred lag, where $\chi^2$ is the error-weighted sum of squared residuals. We therefore scale the reported uncertainties by a common factor, estimated together with $\gamma$ and $r_0$ in the likelihood for each lag, which widens the parameter intervals accordingly. The posterior is then formed as a Gaussian mixture of the fits for each $\delta$, with weights from the Bayesian information criterion. Every candidate lag has the same number of fitted parameters, so these weights reflect only how well each lag fits the data.

Among the candidates, this procedure places 98.0\% of the weight on $\delta = 6$~yr. It gives posterior medians $\gamma = 0.338$ [0.299, 0.376]~mm\,SLE\,yr$^{-1}$\,$^\circ$C$^{-1}$ and $r_0 = 1.389$ [1.376, 1.401]~mm\,SLE\,yr$^{-1}$ (Fig.~S1). The fit reproduces the discharge rate at the end of the record without using that rate separately. Over 2008--2018 the modeled rate is 1.466~mm\,SLE\,yr$^{-1}$, compared with an observed 1.475~mm\,SLE\,yr$^{-1}$, a difference smaller than the uncertainty of the observed rate.

The fitted $\delta = 6$~yr is an effective lag for spatially averaged forcing, and we therefore do not attempt to attribute it to a single process. It lies within the range of timescales spanned by the ocean-to-ice pathway. These timescales include the exchange of water between the continental shelf and the fjords, the response of glacier fronts to undercutting, and the inland spread of retreat to the flux gates, the fixed cross-sections near each glacier front where discharge is measured \citep{StraneoHeimbach2013,Wood2021,robel_response_2018}. Because $T_{\mathrm{ocean}}$ is averaged over Greenland's peripheral waters, $\delta$ also absorbs the time required for a warming anomaly to become representative of conditions at outlet glaciers distributed around the ice sheet.

Equation~\ref{eq:greenland_rate} is linear and reversible, so it does not represent retreat that persists after ocean forcing declines, nor the control of fjord and bed topography on retreat. Glaciers calving on shallow ridges or standing in cold, shallow water have retreated little, while glaciers in deep fjords reached by warm Atlantic Water have retreated substantially \citep{Wood2021}. By summing discharge over all 219 marine-terminating glaciers, we assume that these contrasts average into a single ice-sheet-wide sensitivity, $\gamma$, rather than resolving them glacier by glacier.

We validate the model against two records withheld from calibration, the discharge time series of \citet{Mankoff2021} spanning 1986--2023 and the time series from the Ice Sheet Mass Balance Intercomparison Exercise (IMBIE-3) spanning 1972--2023 \citep{Otosaka2026}. The validation with \citet{Mankoff2021} is informative but weak, because that time series shares its underlying raw data with the \citet{Mouginot2019} calibration and differs only in processing. IMBIE-3 combines three kinds of estimates. Input-output estimates subtract discharge from SMB, and those in IMBIE-3 include \citet{Mouginot2019} and \citet{Mankoff2021}. Altimetry estimates, from satellite measurements of ice-surface height since 1992, and gravimetry estimates, from satellite measurements of Earth's gravity field since 2002, are independent of the calibration. Before 1992, IMBIE-3 rests on input-output estimates alone. Its discharge series is total mass balance minus the mean SMB of three regional climate models, one of which is RACMO, the model that also supplies the \citet{Mouginot2019} SMB. The independent part of the IMBIE-3 test is therefore the total mass balance after 1992.

Our model falls within the uncertainties of the validation data for discharge and total mass balance (Fig.~\ref{fig:greenland_delay_fit}). Because the SMB part of the model is fitted to the \citet{Mouginot2019} SMB (Section~\ref{s:greenland_smb}), the total mass balance mainly tests the discharge model. The SMB emulator alone is tested against observed SMB in the Supplementary Material. The validation results suggest that our simple model, with two fitted parameters and a selected lag, captures cumulative GrIS discharge during the observational record and its sensitivity to ocean temperatures in Greenland's peripheral waters.

To our knowledge, no published study reports a Greenland-wide sensitivity of discharge rate to ocean temperature, so we derive a simple dimensional scaling to contextualize the inferred value $\gamma = 0.338$~mm\,SLE\,yr$^{-1}$\,$^\circ$C$^{-1}$. For a single outlet glacier $i$, the submarine melt rate scales approximately linearly with ocean thermal forcing, the ocean temperature above the in situ freezing point. We treat a change in $T_{\mathrm{ocean}}$ as an equal change in thermal forcing, so that
\begin{equation}
	\Delta \dot{m}_i = \kappa_i\,\Delta T_{\mathrm{ocean}},
	\label{eq:melt_rate}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa_i$ is the melt sensitivity to thermal forcing averaged over the submerged calving face. Plume parameterizations calibrated with Greenland observations \citep{Xu2013, Rignot2016, Slater2022} give a maximum melt sensitivity of 90--260~m\,yr$^{-1}$\,$^\circ$C$^{-1}$ near the grounding line. The face-averaged sensitivity is a half to a third of that value \citep{Wood2021}. Dividing by 2.5, we take $\kappa_i = 36$--$104$~m\,yr$^{-1}$\,$^\circ$C$^{-1}$, with a geometric mean of 61~m\,yr$^{-1}$\,$^\circ$C$^{-1}$. Submarine melt thins and undercuts the terminus, which triggers calving and retreat of the grounding line. The resulting increase in ice flux is larger than the direct submarine melt flux by a factor $\mu$. Neither flux is measured ice-sheet wide, so we take $\mu = 3$--$10$ as an assumed range. For comparison, submarine melt beneath floating termini removes 5--85\% of grounding-line discharge \citep{Enderlin2013}, and ice-sheet projections that exclude ocean-induced undercutting may underestimate mass loss by at least a factor of 2 \citep{Wood2021}. The flux perturbation from a single glacier with submerged calving-front area $A_{\mathrm{f}}$ is then
\begin{equation}
	\Delta \dot{Q}_i = \mu_i\,\rho_{\mathrm{ice}}\,A_{\mathrm{f},i}\,\kappa_i\,\Delta T_{\mathrm{ocean}},
	\label{eq:single_glacier_flux}
\end{equation}
where $\rho_{\mathrm{ice}} = 900$~kg\,m$^{-3}$ is the density of ice. Summing over Greenland's $N = 219$ marine-terminating outlets \citep{Mouginot2019}, we approximate the scale of $\gamma$ as
\begin{equation}
	\widetilde{\gamma} \sim \frac{1}{A_{\mathrm{ocean}}\,\rho_{\mathrm{fw}}\,\Delta T_{\mathrm{ocean}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \Delta\dot{Q}_i ,
	\label{eq:gamma_aggregate}
\end{equation}
where $A_{\mathrm{ocean}} = 3.625 \times 10^{14}$~m$^2$ is the ocean area and $\rho_{\mathrm{fw}} = 1000$~kg\,m$^{-3}$ is the density of freshwater. Together, these convert added ice mass to SLE. Submerged calving-front areas of 0.9--2.7~km$^2$ are reported for five West Greenland outlets \citep{Rignot2016}, and we take this range as representative of all 219 outlets. Evaluating Eq.~\ref{eq:gamma_aggregate} across the full parameter ranges gives
\begin{equation}
	\widetilde{\gamma} = 0.05\text{--}1.5~\mathrm{mm\,SLE\,yr}^{-1}\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}^{-1},
	\label{eq:gamma_estimate}
\end{equation}
which contains our calibrated $\gamma = 0.338$~mm\,SLE\,yr$^{-1}$\,$^\circ$C$^{-1}$. We report Eq.~\ref{eq:gamma_estimate} only as an order-of-magnitude check on the inferred value, to build intuition. The range spans a factor of 29 because it multiplies three uncertain quantities, $\mu$, $A_{\mathrm{f}}$, and $\kappa$, none of which is measured across the whole ice sheet. The estimate also treats these quantities as uncorrelated across outlets. In fact, the deepest and widest fronts sit in the warmest fjords, which would push the estimate upward. Conversely, two of the five glaciers with measured front areas are among the fastest in Greenland \citep{Rignot2016}, so the adopted areas likely exceed the mean over all 219 outlets, most of which are small.

Surface meltwater that drains beneath glaciers strengthens the plumes that melt their fronts, and this runoff rises along with ocean temperature. The calibrated $\gamma$ is therefore an effective sensitivity that also captures part of the atmospheric influence on discharge. At the ice-sheet scale, variability in submarine melting driven by the atmosphere accounts for about 30\% of modeled retreat and dynamic sea-level contribution \citep{Slater2022}.

\subsubsubsection{Surface mass balance}
\label{s:greenland_smb}

For Greenland SMB, the forecast uses a statistical emulator of the regional climate model MARv3.12. The training simulations, produced by \citet{Boberg2026}, are MAR runs driven by seven global climate models of the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6). For each global model, we smooth its GMST anomaly $T$ relative to 1995--2005 with an 11-yr centered mean. We then fit the annual SMB contribution as a quadratic in the smoothed anomaly with first-order autoregressive residuals, $\dot{H}_{\mathrm{smb}} = a_{\mathrm{gris}}T^2 + b_{\mathrm{gris}}T + c_{\mathrm{gris}}$, pooling its historical and SSP simulations (Supplementary Material, Figs.~S6--S8). Each Monte Carlo member draws one global model, with equal weight, and one posterior draw of $a_{\mathrm{gris}}$ and $b_{\mathrm{gris}}$. Because MAR is the only regional climate model in the training set, the spread between regional climate models is not sampled \citep{Glaude2024}. When each global model is left out in turn, the other six predict its 2015--2100 SMB contribution within the 90\% predictive range for all 13 withheld simulations.

The observed SMB departs slowly from the emulator's response to observed warming. Relative to 1995--2005, the \citet{Mouginot2019} SMB lies about 80~Gt\,yr$^{-1}$ above the emulator over 1972--1985 and 106~Gt\,yr$^{-1}$ below it over 2010--2018 (Supplementary Material). We represent this departure as a drift state $s(t)$ that relaxes toward zero over a timescale $\tau_{\mathrm{s}}$, so that $\dot{H}_{\mathrm{smb}} = a_{\mathrm{gris}}T^2 + b_{\mathrm{gris}}T + c_{\mathrm{gris}} + s(t) + \varepsilon(t)$, where $\varepsilon$ is year-to-year weather. For each member, $c_{\mathrm{gris}}$ and one path of $s$ are drawn from their Kalman-filter posterior given the \citet{Mouginot2019} SMB over 1972--2018. The 47-year record does not constrain $\tau_{\mathrm{s}}$. We therefore give it a lognormal prior whose 90\% range is 5 to 25~yr, which allows about two relaxation times within the record, and obtain $\tau_{\mathrm{s}} = 12$ [6, 26]~yr. In 2018 the observed SMB minus the emulator is $-24$ [$-157$, 124]~Gt\,yr$^{-1}$. After 2018 the state continues as a first-order autoregressive process whose mean relaxes toward zero. The drift state does not change the emulator's response to warming. Figure~\ref{fig:greenland_delay_fit}c shows the emulated SMB, including the drift state, added to the modeled discharge and compared with the observed total mass balance.

We also include the SMB--elevation feedback, which adds $8 \pm 5$\% (1$\sigma$, with negative draws set to zero) to SMB loss in the reference simulation of \citet{Fettweis2013}. We scale this fraction by each member's cumulative SMB anomaly since 2000.

\subsubsection{Thermosteric}

The ocean is the dominant reservoir for the excess heat accumulated by the climate system \citep{Cheng2017}, and the resulting thermal expansion of seawater, the thermosteric contribution, is currently the largest single contributor to observed sea-level rise \citep{Frederikse2020}. A simple model of thermal expansion must represent both heat exchange between the ocean and the atmosphere and the transport of heat within the ocean. The ocean warms with a delay relative to the atmosphere. Heat enters at the sea surface and is carried downward by turbulent mixing and by the overturning circulation, so the temperature of the interior reflects the history of surface warming. Downward transfer spans a wide range of timescales. The upper few hundred meters exchange heat with the atmosphere over years to decades, whereas water below the thermocline is renewed over centuries. We therefore represent the ocean as two layers, a fast upper layer coupled to the atmosphere and a slow deep layer coupled only to the upper layer, following the structure of \citet{Geoffroy2013}. This structure reproduces both the lag between surface warming and expansion and the continued expansion that follows a stabilization of surface temperature, because the deep layer continues to draw heat from the upper layer after the upper layer has equilibrated.

Our model combines elements of earlier simple models. Its structure follows two-layer energy-balance models of the climate system \citep{Gregory2000,Held2010,Geoffroy2013}, in which a fast upper layer exchanges heat with the atmosphere and a slow deep layer exchanges heat with the upper layer. Those models are driven by radiative forcing. Ours is driven by GMST, as are semi-empirical models in which sea level relaxes toward an equilibrium set by temperature \citep{Grinsted2010,Mengel2016,Wong2017}. Radiative forcing enters our model only through a term for volcanic eruptions, because GMST does not capture their effect on the ocean. In the semi-empirical models the thermosteric contribution has a single relaxation time, 82 to 1290~yr in \citet{Mengel2016}, and an equilibrium sensitivity taken from long-term model integrations. In our model the delay acts on the thermal state of each layer rather than on sea level, and sea level follows from the thermal state through the equation of state of seawater, as in the layered thermal expansion of the MAGICC sea-level model \citep{Nauels2017}. Models driven by ocean heat content instead convert heat to sea level with an expansion efficiency that is fixed in time \citep{Kuhlbrodt2012,Fox-Kemper2021}.

Multi-layer energy-balance models driven by radiative forcing have been calibrated against observed ocean heat content in separate depth ranges \citep{Skeie2014,Nadiga2019}. We apply this depth-resolved calibration to our GMST-driven model. Each layer is compared directly with the observed thermosteric record for its own depth range, the upper layer with 0--700\,m and the deep layer with 700--2000\,m.

Consider first an upper layer of thickness $h_{\mathrm{u}} = 700$~m warmed by $\Delta\theta(z,t)$. The resulting rise in the steric height of the ocean is
	\begin{equation}
	H_{\mathrm{u}} = \int_0^{h_{\mathrm{u}}}\!\int_0^{\Delta\theta} \alpha_{\mathrm{sw}}\,d\theta'\,dz,
	\label{eq:steric_integral}
	\end{equation}
where $\alpha_{\mathrm{sw}}(z)$ is the thermal expansion coefficient of seawater, which varies with depth because it depends on temperature and pressure. The temperature anomaly profile is defined as $\Delta\theta = S_{\mathrm{u}}\varphi$, where $S_{\mathrm{u}}(t)$ is the amplitude of the anomaly and $\varphi(z)$ is a dimensionless shape factor. Expanding $\alpha_{\mathrm{sw}} = \alpha_{0,\mathrm{sw}}(z) + \alpha'_{\mathrm{sw}}\theta'$, with $\alpha'_{\mathrm{sw}} = \partial\alpha_{\mathrm{sw}}/\partial\theta$, we rewrite Eq.~\ref{eq:steric_integral} as 
	\begin{equation}
	H_{\mathrm{u}} = a_{\mathrm{u}} S_{\mathrm{u}}^2 + b_{\mathrm{u}} S_{\mathrm{u}},
	\label{eq:thermo_upper}
	\end{equation}
where $a_{\mathrm{u}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\int_0^{h_{\mathrm{u}}} f\alpha'_{\mathrm{sw}}\varphi^2dz$ and $b_{\mathrm{u}} = \int_0^{h_{\mathrm{u}}} f\alpha_{0,\mathrm{sw}}\varphi dz$, and $f(z)$ is the ocean area at depth $z$ as a fraction of the ocean surface area. The coefficient $b_{\mathrm{u}}$ sets the expansion of the upper layer per degree of warming, and $a_{\mathrm{u}}$ sets how that expansion grows with warming, because seawater expands more per degree when it is warmer. We do not fit $a_{\mathrm{u}}$. For an anomaly profile of fixed shape, $\varphi = A\,g(z)$ with a constant $A$, the two integrals give $a_{\mathrm{u}} = \kappa_{\mathrm{sw}} b_{\mathrm{u}}^2$, where $\kappa_{\mathrm{sw}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\int_0^{h_{\mathrm{u}}} f\alpha'_{\mathrm{sw}}g^2dz \big/ \bigl(\int_0^{h_{\mathrm{u}}} f\alpha_{0,\mathrm{sw}}\,g\,dz\bigr)^2$ depends only on the properties of seawater and the shape of the ocean basins. We compute $\kappa_{\mathrm{sw}} = 0.265$~m$^{-1}$ from the TEOS-10 equation of state \citep{IOC2010} and the 1995--2005 mean temperature and salinity of EN4 \citep{Good2013}, for a profile that is uniform over 0--700~m. In each posterior sample, $a_{\mathrm{u}}$ then follows from $b_{\mathrm{u}}$. As GMST, $T(t)$, changes, the amplitude evolves as
	\begin{equation}
	\frac{dS_{\mathrm{u}}}{dt} = \frac{T - S_{\mathrm{u}}}{\tau_{\mathrm{u}}} + k_{\mathrm{v}}F_{\mathrm{v}},
	\label{eq:thermo_su}
	\end{equation}
where $\tau_{\mathrm{u}}$ is the characteristic time over which the thermal state $S_{\mathrm{u}}$ relaxes toward GMST, and the second term represents volcanic eruptions, as described below. We scale $\varphi$ so that $S_{\mathrm{u}} = T$ at equilibrium, which places the ratio of upper-ocean warming to GMST in $b_{\mathrm{u}}$. Both $S_{\mathrm{u}}$ and $T$ are anomalies from the 1995--2005 mean. Without the volcanic term, Eq.~\ref{eq:thermo_su} shows that $S_{\mathrm{u}}$ is an average of past temperatures, weighted toward the recent past over a time window set by $\tau_{\mathrm{u}}$. The upper layer therefore smooths year-to-year fluctuations in GMST and follows longer trends with a delay comparable to $\tau_{\mathrm{u}}$.

GMST alone does not capture the response of the upper ocean to volcanic eruptions. After a large eruption, stratospheric aerosol reduces the sunlight absorbed at the sea surface. Ocean heat content and sea level fall within about a year and recover over a decade or more \citep{Church2005}, and part of the cooling reaches deeper layers, where it persists for decades \citep{Gleckler2006}. GMST recovers within a few years, so a model driven by GMST alone misses most of this heat deficit. The second term in Eq.~\ref{eq:thermo_su} supplies this deficit, with $F_{\mathrm{v}}(t)$ the volcanic effective radiative forcing of \citet{Forster2025}. This forcing is an anomaly relative to the mean over 500 BCE to 1749 \citep{Forster2021}, and its mean over 1850--2024 is close to zero. Energy conservation sets the coefficient, $k_{\mathrm{v}} = m_{\mathrm{v}}c_{\mathrm{v}}/b_{\mathrm{u}}$. Here $c_{\mathrm{v}} = 1.57$~mm per W\,m$^{-2}$\,yr is the 0--700\,m thermosteric rise produced when a forcing of 1~W\,m$^{-2}$ over the ocean, 71\% of the Earth's surface, is sustained for one year. This value uses the expansion efficiency of the NOAA 0--700\,m record, 0.137~m\,YJ$^{-1}$ (1~YJ $= 10^{24}$~J), the ratio of the thermosteric trend to the heat content trend over 1955--2010 \citep{Levitus2012}. Dividing by $b_{\mathrm{u}}$ converts sea level to the thermal state $S_{\mathrm{u}}$.

The multiplier $m_{\mathrm{v}}$ has the prior $\mathcal{N}(1, 0.19^2)$, where $\mathcal{N}$ denotes a normal distribution, here with a mean of 1 and a standard deviation of 0.19. This prior combines the $\pm$25\% uncertainty (5--95\%) of the volcanic forcing \citep{Forster2021} with the 12\% uncertainty (one standard deviation) of the AR6 expansion efficiency \citep{Fox-Kemper2021}. The forcing is zero after 2024, as in the AR6 scenarios, so the term affects the projections only through the thermal state reached by 2024. For 2022--2024 it includes a small net positive forcing from the eruption of Hunga Tonga--Hunga Ha'apai, because the warming effect of the water vapor injected into the stratosphere outweighs the cooling effect of the aerosol, although \citet{Forster2025} note that other studies find this net forcing to be negative or close to zero.

The deep layer follows the same construction, with $\Delta\theta_{\mathrm{d}}(z,t) = S_{\mathrm{d}}(t)\,\varphi_{\mathrm{d}}(z)$ over 700--2000~m depth and $b_{\mathrm{d}} = \int_{h_{\mathrm{u}}}^{h_{\mathrm{d}}} \alpha_{0,\mathrm{sw}}\,\varphi_{\mathrm{d}}\,dz$, with $h_{\mathrm{d}} = 2000$~m. We drop the quadratic term because $S_{\mathrm{d}}$ lags well behind $S_{\mathrm{u}}$ ($\tau_{\mathrm{d}} \gg \tau_{\mathrm{u}}$). At the posterior median $\tau_{\mathrm{u}}$, $S_{\mathrm{d}}$ rises about 1.0 $^\circ$C above its 1995--2005 level by 2100 in the 3.6 $^\circ$C scenario, compared with about 3.0 $^\circ$C for $S_{\mathrm{u}}$. The 20-year 700--2000~m record also cannot constrain a second quadratic coefficient. Warming from GMST reaches the deep layer only after passing through the upper layer, so the thermal state $S_{\mathrm{d}}$ relaxes toward $S_{\mathrm{u}}$ as
      \begin{equation}
      \frac{dS_{\mathrm{d}}}{dt} = \frac{S_{\mathrm{u}} - S_{\mathrm{d}}}{\tau_{\mathrm{d}}},
      \label{eq:thermo_sd}
      \end{equation}
where $\tau_{\mathrm{d}}$ is the relaxation time of the deep layer. The 20-year 700--2000\,m record is too short to constrain a centennial relaxation time, so $\tau_{\mathrm{d}}$ has a lognormal prior fitted to the ratio of deep-ocean heat capacity to the heat-exchange coefficient in the 16 CMIP5 models of \citet[][Table 4]{Geoffroy2013}, with a median of 133~yr and a 90\% range of 62 to 288~yr. Adding this layer gives the total thermosteric response for the upper 2000~m as
      \begin{equation}
      H_{\mathrm{ud}}(t) = H_{\mathrm{u}}(t) + b_{\mathrm{d}}\,S_{\mathrm{d}}.
      \label{eq:thermo_total}
      \end{equation}
Finally, to account for expansion below 2000~m depth, we define 
	\begin{equation}
	H_{\mathrm{full}}(t) = H_{\mathrm{ud}}(t) + \dot{H}_{\mathrm{b}}(t - t_{\mathrm{ref}}),
	\label{eq:thermo_full}
	\end{equation}
where $\dot{H}_{\mathrm{b}} = 0.12 \pm 0.04$\,mm\,yr$^{-1}$ (one standard deviation, 1991--2010) \citep{Desbruyeres2016} is a constant rate for depths below 2000\,m, referenced to year $t_{\mathrm{ref}} = 2000$. This layer is not observed at annual resolution, so its rate is adopted rather than calibrated.

We force the model with the Berkeley Earth GMST record \citep{Rohde2020} and the volcanic forcing, and fit it jointly to the NOAA thermosteric sea-level estimates \citep{Levitus2012} at 0--700\,m (1955--2024), compared with $H_{\mathrm{u}}$, and at 700--2000\,m (2005--2024), compared with $b_{\mathrm{d}}S_{\mathrm{d}}$. The 700--2000\,m record is the difference between the 0--2000\,m and 0--700\,m records in the same years. We fit this difference rather than the 0--2000\,m record because the 0--2000\,m record contains the 0--700\,m data, which would otherwise enter the fit twice over 2005--2024. We multiply both NOAA records by 1.14 to correct the low bias identified by \citet{Li2022}.

We use Bayesian inference \citep{Tarantola2005} in which Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling draws parameter combinations $\boldsymbol{\phi} = (b_{\mathrm{u}}, b_{\mathrm{d}}, \tau_{\mathrm{u}}, \tau_{\mathrm{d}}, m_{\mathrm{v}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{extra,u}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{extra,d}}, \rho)$ with probability density proportional to the posterior (Fig.~S2 of the Supplementary Material). For each record $j \in \{\mathrm{u}, \mathrm{d}\}$, $\mathbf{r}_j(\boldsymbol{\phi})$ is the vector of residuals after subtracting the respective means from the record and model over the same years. This removes each record's arbitrary reference level. We assume that the residuals are normally distributed about the model with covariance
      \begin{equation}
      \begin{aligned}
      \mathbf{C}_j &= \mathrm{diag}\bigl(\sigma_{j,i}^2\bigr) + \sigma_{\mathrm{extra},j}^2\,\mathbf{R}_j, \\
      (\mathbf{R}_j)_{ik} &= \rho^{|t_i - t_k|},
      \end{aligned}
      \label{eq:thermo_cov}
      \end{equation}
where $\sigma_{j,i}$ is the observational error of point $i$ in year $t_i$, the quadrature sum of the formal measurement error and a structural uncertainty equal to 5\% of the bias-corrected anomaly. These errors are independent between years. The noise term $\sigma_{\mathrm{extra},j}$ absorbs misfit beyond the reported observational error, separately for each record. It represents variability in ocean heat content that the model does not resolve and that persists from one year to the next, so we correlate it between years as a first-order autoregressive process with coefficient $\rho$, shared by both records.

The relaxation time of the upper layer, $\tau_{\mathrm{u}}$, has an informative prior, $\ln\tau_{\mathrm{u}} \sim \mathcal{N}(\ln 8, 0.5^2)$ with $\tau_{\mathrm{u}}$ in years, so that $\mu_{\tau_{\mathrm{u}}} = \ln 8$ and $s_{\tau_{\mathrm{u}}} = 0.5$. The prior median is 8~yr and its 90\% interval spans 3.5 to 18~yr, consistent with the ratio of upper-layer heat capacity to the heat-exchange coefficient, which spans 4 to 17~yr across the CMIP5 models in \citet[][Table 4]{Geoffroy2013}, with a multi-model mean of 10~yr. The prior on $\tau_{\mathrm{d}}$ has $\mu_{\tau_{\mathrm{d}}} = \ln 133$ and $s_{\tau_{\mathrm{d}}} = 0.47$, and the prior on $m_{\mathrm{v}}$ has standard deviation $s_{m} = 0.19$. Adding the negative log-priors gives the objective function
      \begin{equation}
      \mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\phi}) = \sum_{j} \left[\mathbf{r}_j^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{C}_j^{-1}\mathbf{r}_j + \ln\det\mathbf{C}_j\right] + \left(\frac{\ln\tau_{\mathrm{u}} - \mu_{\tau_{\mathrm{u}}}}{s_{\tau_{\mathrm{u}}}}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\ln\tau_{\mathrm{d}} - \mu_{\tau_{\mathrm{d}}}}{s_{\tau_{\mathrm{d}}}}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{m_{\mathrm{v}} - 1}{s_{m}}\right)^2,
      \label{eq:thermo_objective}
      \end{equation}
which is twice the negative log-posterior up to an additive constant. The $\ln\det\mathbf{C}_j$ term remains because $\mathbf{C}_j$ depends on the sampled $\sigma_{\mathrm{extra},j}$ and $\rho$. The parameters $b_{\mathrm{u}}$ and $b_{\mathrm{d}}$ have flat priors constrained to be non-negative, $\ln\sigma_{\mathrm{extra,u}}$ has a flat prior, and $\rho$ has a flat prior on $(-0.99, 0.99)$. The 700--2000\,m residuals lie mostly within the reported errors, so the likelihood remains finite as $\sigma_{\mathrm{extra,d}}$ approaches zero, where a prior flat in its logarithm would be improper. We therefore use a prior that is flat in $\sigma_{\mathrm{extra,d}}$ itself, on (0, 10]~mm.

The MCMC sampling uses the emcee package with 64 walkers, 6000 burn-in steps, and 30,000 production steps. The longest integrated autocorrelation time is 215 steps, about 1/140 of the chain length, giving at least 8900 effective samples per parameter.

The parameters $b_{\mathrm{u}}$ and $\tau_{\mathrm{u}}$ are partly degenerate in the likelihood, because a slower upper-layer response can be offset by a larger sensitivity to fit the same trajectory. This degeneracy is broken by $\tau_{\mathrm{u}}$'s informative prior.

The posterior medians and 90\% credible intervals are $b_{\mathrm{u}} = 42.3$ [36.9, 51.1] mm $^\circ$C$^{-1}$, which gives $a_{\mathrm{u}} = 0.47$ [0.36, 0.69] mm $^\circ$C$^{-2}$, $b_{\mathrm{d}} = 70$ [38, 139] mm $^\circ$C$^{-1}$, $\tau_{\mathrm{u}} = 12.2$ [7.2, 21.9] yr, $\tau_{\mathrm{d}} = 157$ [71, 345] yr, $m_{\mathrm{v}} = 1.05$ [0.79, 1.30], $\sigma_{\mathrm{extra,u}} = 1.81$ [1.43, 2.51] mm, $\sigma_{\mathrm{extra,d}} = 0.45$ [0.11, 0.87] mm, and $\rho = 0.60$ [0.37, 0.80] (Fig.~S2). The posterior of $m_{\mathrm{v}}$ is only slightly narrower than its prior, so the size of the volcanic term is set mainly by energy conservation rather than by the fit. The calibrated model reproduces both records with root-mean-square (RMS) residuals of 1.99 mm for 0--700\,m and 0.61 mm for 700--2000\,m, compared with 40 and 7.2 mm of observed steric rise over the respective records (Fig.~\ref{fig:ocean_fit_residuals}).


We validate the full-depth model in Eq.~\ref{eq:thermo_full} against two global-mean steric sea-level records, both withheld from calibration. \citet{Frederikse2020} reconstruct steric sea level for 1900--2018. Over 1957--2018 they draw, for each ensemble member, from three gridded products built on the same archive of in situ temperature and salinity profiles \citep{Levitus2012, Ishii2017, Cheng2016}. The first of these supplies the data used to calibrate our model, so over this period the comparison measures consistency between separate analyses of largely shared data. Before 1957, in situ coverage is too sparse to resolve global steric change, and \citet{Frederikse2020} instead use the \citet{Zanna2019} reconstruction of ocean heat storage. That reconstruction is inferred from sea-surface temperature anomalies and heat-transport pathways in an ocean reanalysis. The \citet{Zanna2019} reconstruction also supplies the expansion below 2000~m in \citet{Frederikse2020} over the full 1900--2018 period, whereas our model uses the constant rate of \citet{Desbruyeres2016} for this layer. The pre-1957 segment is therefore the part of this comparison that is independent of the data used in calibration. The RMS difference between our model and the reconstruction is 2.97 mm over 1957--2018 and 5.00 mm before 1957 (Fig.~\ref{fig:ocean_fit_residuals}c,f).

Our second validation comes from \citet{Dangendorf2024}, who assimilate 516 tide-gauge records and satellite altimetry in an ensemble Kalman smoother that solves for GMSL over 1900--2021. Their global-mean steric estimate is what remains of GMSL after prescribed barystatic \citep[from][]{Frederikse2020}, glacial isostatic adjustment, and inverse-barometer contributions are removed. No temperature or salinity measurement enters this calculation, so the comparison tests our model against a steric estimate that shares none of the in situ ocean data used to calibrate it. The RMS difference between our model and their reconstruction is 3.65 mm over 1957--2021 and 11.44 mm before 1957.

\subsubsection{Glaciers}

Projected 21st-century glacier mass loss, and its end-of-century rate, scale approximately linearly with global warming \citep{Rounce2023,Edwards2021}. We therefore model the rate of the glacier contribution to sea level as linear in GMST,
\begin{equation}
	\dot{H}_{\mathrm{gl}} = b_{\mathrm{gl}}T + c_{\mathrm{gl}},
	\label{eq:glacier_rate}
\end{equation}
where $T$ is the calendar-year mean Berkeley Earth GMST anomaly relative to 1995--2005 \citep{Rohde2020}, $b_{\mathrm{gl}}$ is the sensitivity of the loss rate to warming, and $c_{\mathrm{gl}}$ is the loss rate at the 1995--2005 mean GMST. To treat uncertainty consistently across components, we calibrate the model by Bayesian inference against the annual global glacier mass changes of the Glacier Mass Balance Intercomparison Exercise (GlaMBIE) for 2000--2023 \citep{GlaMBIE2025}. The GlaMBIE global total sums all 19 regions of the Randolph Glacier Inventory, including the peripheral glaciers of Greenland and Antarctica. Because the Greenland SMB emulator covers only the contiguous ice sheet (Section~\ref{s:greenland_smb}), the Greenland peripheral glaciers enter the projections only through this component. The Antarctic peripheral glaciers in GlaMBIE's global total (0.05 mm yr$^{-1}$ over 2000--2023) are partly included in the IMBIE-3 gravimetry estimates for Antarctica as well, so the two records may overlap by up to that amount. We fit the time-integrated form of Eq.~\ref{eq:glacier_rate}, which gives the cumulative contribution,
\begin{equation}
	H_{\mathrm{gl}}(t) = b_{\mathrm{gl}}\int_{t_0}^{t}T(t')\,\mathrm{d}t' + c_{\mathrm{gl}}(t - t_0) + H_{\mathrm{gl},0} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{gl}}(t),
	\label{eq:glacier_level}
\end{equation}
where $t_0 = 2000$ is the baseline year and $H_{\mathrm{gl},0}$ is the modeled contribution in that year. The observational error $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{gl}}$ is normally distributed with covariance $\Sigma_{\mathrm{gl}}$. Summing annual mass changes into a cumulative record correlates its errors in time, and $\Sigma_{\mathrm{gl}}$ is the covariance of the cumulative record implied by independent annual errors. GlaMBIE reports its uncertainties as 95\% intervals, which we convert to standard deviations. The residuals scatter more widely than these uncertainties allow, with a reduced chi-square of 4.1, so we add a model-inadequacy term $\sigma_{\mathrm{gl,extra}}$ in quadrature to each annual uncertainty and fit it with the other parameters.

We apply a weakly informative prior $b_{\mathrm{gl}} \sim \mathcal{N}(0.61,\,2.0^2)$~mm\,yr$^{-1}$\,$^\circ$C$^{-1}$, truncated to $b_{\mathrm{gl}} \geq 0$. The prior mean is taken from \citet{Rounce2023}, whose end-of-century rates of glacier sea-level rise increase from 0.70 to 2.23~mm\,yr$^{-1}$ as warming above pre-industrial increases from 1.5 to 4~$^\circ$C, a slope of 0.61~mm\,yr$^{-1}$\,$^\circ$C$^{-1}$. Widening the standard deviation fiftyfold leaves the posterior unchanged. Truncating the prior at zero recognizes that glaciers lose mass as the climate warms.

Left free, $c_{\mathrm{gl}}$ makes the fitted model gain glacier mass in most years before 1950 and in 18 of the 30 years from 1950 to 1979. In reality, glaciers entered the industrial era close to balance with the slowly cooling climate of the preceding centuries \citep{Roe2021}. We therefore require the loss rate to be zero at a balance temperature $T_{\mathrm{gl,eq}}$, which gives $c_{\mathrm{gl}} = -b_{\mathrm{gl}}T_{\mathrm{gl,eq}}$. The model we fit is
\begin{equation}
	H_{\mathrm{gl}}(t) = b_{\mathrm{gl}}\int_{t_0}^{t}\left[T(t') - T_{\mathrm{gl,eq}}\right]\mathrm{d}t' + H_{\mathrm{gl},0} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{gl}}(t).
	\label{eq:glacier_balance}
\end{equation}
The prior on $T_{\mathrm{gl,eq}}$ is normal, centered on the 1850--1879 mean GMST of $-0.86$~$^\circ$C relative to 1995--2005, with a standard deviation of 0.15~$^\circ$C. Equation~\ref{eq:glacier_balance} neglects the adjustment of glacier geometry to climate, which sets each glacier's response time \citep{Roe2021}, so it represents the limit of long response times.

The MCMC sampling uses the emcee package with 32 walkers, 2000 burn-in steps, and 4000 production steps. The posterior medians are $b_{\mathrm{gl}} = 0.76$ [0.60, 0.99]~mm\,yr$^{-1}$\,$^\circ$C$^{-1}$, $T_{\mathrm{gl,eq}} = -0.78$ [$-1.02$, $-0.56$]~$^\circ$C, $c_{\mathrm{gl}} = 0.59$ [0.52, 0.67]~mm\,yr$^{-1}$, and $\sigma_{\mathrm{gl,extra}} = 0.18$ [0.13, 0.26]~mm\,yr$^{-1}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:component_fit_diagnostics}a). With $c_{\mathrm{gl}}$ free, the fit gives $b_{\mathrm{gl}} = 0.99$ [0.57, 1.45]~mm\,yr$^{-1}$\,$^\circ$C$^{-1}$ and $c_{\mathrm{gl}} = 0.53$ [0.41, 0.66]~mm\,yr$^{-1}$. The 2000--2023 record spans only about 0.8~$^\circ$C of GMST, so it constrains the present loss rate more tightly than the sensitivity. The balance constraint adds information from the pre-industrial climate, lowering the median of $b_{\mathrm{gl}}$ by about a quarter and narrows its interval.

We validate the glacier model against the global glacier mass changes of \citet{Zemp2019} for 1962--1999, which are derived from glaciological and geodetic observations and are not used in the calibration. Over these 38 years the model gives a mean rate of 0.30 [0.17, 0.40]~mm\,yr$^{-1}$, compared with an observed 0.32 [$-0.02$, 0.66]~mm\,yr$^{-1}$, where the observed interval is the 90\% range derived from the 95\% uncertainties reported by \citet{Zemp2019} (Fig.~\ref{fig:component_fit_diagnostics}a). Because the model with $c_{\mathrm{gl}}$ free predicts glacier mass gain in most years before the late 1970s, this comparison also tests the balance constraint. We compare only the years before 2000. Over 2000--2016, when both records exist, the loss rate of \citet{Zemp2019} exceeds that of GlaMBIE by 0.18~mm\,yr$^{-1}$, so a comparison there would measure the disagreement between the two records rather than the skill of the model.

\subsubsection{Antarctic Peninsula}
\label{s:peninsula}

The Antarctic Peninsula component uses the same Bayesian framework for cumulative contributions as the glacier component and is fitted at annual resolution to the IMBIE-3 Peninsula time series spanning 1979--2023 (45 annual points; \citealp{Otosaka2026}). We choose the linear rate--temperature model $\dot{H}_{\mathrm{p}} = b_{\mathrm{p}}T + c_{\mathrm{p}}$, which captures the combined response of SMB and ice discharge to warming. IMBIE-3 attributes the Peninsula mass loss over 1979--2023 to ice discharge \citep{Otosaka2026}, which increased after the Larsen A and B ice shelves collapsed in response to regional atmospheric warming \citep{Scambos2004}. As for glaciers, the cumulative form includes an initial level $H_{\mathrm{p},0}$, the model level at the start of the record (1979), fitted with the other parameters, and the likelihood uses the covariance of this cumulative record implied by independent annual IMBIE-3 errors. As for glaciers, we also fit a model-inadequacy term $\sigma_{\mathrm{p,extra}}$ to account for annual scatter beyond the IMBIE-3 reported uncertainty, which lowers the reduced chi-square from 2.63 to 1.00. The priors are $b_{\mathrm{p}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\,2.0^2)$ mm yr$^{-1}$ $^\circ$C$^{-1}$ and $c_{\mathrm{p}} \sim \mathcal{N}(0.3,\,1^2)$ mm yr$^{-1}$. The MCMC sampling uses the emcee package with 32 walkers, 2000 burn-in steps, and 4000 production steps. The posterior median sensitivity is $b_{\mathrm{p}} = 0.045$ [$-0.022$, 0.107] mm yr$^{-1}$ $^\circ$C$^{-1}$, and the rate at the 1995--2005 mean GMST is $c_{\mathrm{p}} = 0.056$ [0.038, 0.072] mm yr$^{-1}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:component_fit_diagnostics}b). The 90\% credible interval for $b_{\mathrm{p}}$ spans zero, so these data do not, on their own, resolve the sign of the temperature sensitivity. 


\subsubsection{East Antarctica}
\label{s:eais}

We fit the linear model $\dot{H}_{\mathrm{e}} = b_{\mathrm{e}}T + c_{\mathrm{e}}$ to the IMBIE-3 East Antarctic Ice Sheet (EAIS) record (1979--2023, 45 annual points; \citealp{Otosaka2026}) in the same framework for cumulative contributions as the Peninsula, with a weak prior $b_{\mathrm{e}} \sim \mathcal{N}(-0.17,\,0.5^2)$ mm yr$^{-1}$ $^\circ$C$^{-1}$ and no model-inadequacy term (reduced chi-square 0.90). The posterior median sensitivity is $b_{\mathrm{e}} = -0.028$ [$-0.160$, 0.102] mm yr$^{-1}$ $^\circ$C$^{-1}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:component_fit_diagnostics}c). This interval spans zero, so the record does not constrain the sensitivity. The forecast instead adopts the snowfall-driven mass gain of $C_T = 60\pm20$ Gt\,yr$^{-1}$ per $^\circ$C of GMST \citep{Frieler2015,Ligtenberg2013}, which converts at 362.5 Gt per mm to $-0.17$ mm yr$^{-1}$ $^\circ$C$^{-1}$ of sea level and also sets the prior mean (Section~\ref{s:gmst_dependence}). 

\subsubsection{West Antarctica}

For WAIS, we do not fit a rate--temperature regression over the observational record as we do for glaciers, the Antarctic Peninsula, and EAIS. Ocean heat has driven most WAIS mass loss, and ocean observations are too sparse to support a fit to ocean temperature. We instead fit the 1979--2023 IMBIE-3 record \citep{Otosaka2026} with a rate that changes linearly in time, so the cumulative contribution is quadratic in time. As for the Antarctic Peninsula, the likelihood is that of the annual increments of the cumulative record, each with the reported IMBIE-3 uncertainty plus a fitted model-inadequacy term. The acceleration $a_{\mathrm{w}}$ has a zero-mean prior, $a_{\mathrm{w}} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\,0.05^2)$ mm yr$^{-2}$. The fit yields an acceleration of 0.0122 [0.0099, 0.0143] mm yr$^{-2}$ and a rate in year 2000 of 0.247 [0.219, 0.273] mm yr$^{-1}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:component_fit_diagnostics}d). \citet{Davison2023} report snowfall anomalies in the Amundsen Sea Embayment over 2009--2013 and 2019--2020 that modulate this record. Removing them lowers the extrapolated contribution at 2100 from 86 to 79~mm, well within the uncertainty of the continued-trend scenario (S1) described below, so we fit the record as reported. We set the WAIS rate to zero before 1979, when the IMBIE-3 record begins.


\subsection{Forecasting methods}
\label{s:forecasting_model}

This section describes how the forecast joins the observed satellite-era trend to the component models, how uncertainty is propagated, and how each component is linked to projected GMST. The forecasts presented here use the IPCC AR6 GMST trajectories for different emissions scenarios, though the framework can be applied to any GMST trajectory. We focus on the policy-relevant emissions scenarios, labeled by their AR6 central-estimate warming over 2081--2100: 1.8, 2.7, and 3.6 $^\circ$C. We initialize the GMSL forecast using the GMSL level, rate, and acceleration observed in the satellite era and blend the observed rate with the rate from the component-model sum, such that
\begin{equation}
	\dot{H}_{\mathrm{forecast}} = w\dot{H}_{\mathrm{obs}} + \bigl[1 - w\bigr]\dot{H}_{\mathrm{comp}},
	\label{eq:blend_rate}
\end{equation}
where $\dot{H}_{\mathrm{obs}}$ is the rate of our quadratic fit to the NASA satellite altimetry record over 1993--2025, following \citet{Nerem2022} and \citet{Hamlington2024}, and $\dot{H}_{\mathrm{comp}}$ is the rate of the summed component projections. The blending weight $w(t)$ declines smoothly from 1 to 0,
\begin{equation}
	w = 1 - \Theta\left(\frac{t - t_{\mathrm{r}}}{\tau_{\mathrm{r}}}\right),
	\label{eq:sigmoid_weight}
\end{equation}
where $\Theta(x) =(1 + e^{-x})^{-1}$ is the standard logistic function, $t_{\mathrm{r}} = 2035$, and $\tau_{\mathrm{r}} = 5$ yr. At the forecast origin in 2025, $w = 0.87$. At $t \ll t_{\mathrm{r}}$, $w \approx 1$ and the forecast follows the observed quadratic trajectory. At $t \gg t_{\mathrm{r}}$, $w \approx 0$ and the forecast follows the component models. The weight falls from 0.9 to 0.1 over $t_{\mathrm{r}} \pm 2.2\tau_{\mathrm{r}}$, a span of about 22 yr centered on 2035. We chose $t_{\mathrm{r}} = 2035$, 10 yr after the forecast origin. This interval is about one third of the length of the 1993--2025 satellite altimetry record, a horizon over which the extrapolation of observations should have strong forecasting skill. This taper reflects the expected decline in the forecasting skill of extrapolated observations over timescales longer than roughly half of the observational window. Moving $t_{\mathrm{r}}$ to 2040, 15 yr after the forecast origin or about half the length of the altimetry record and arguably the latest viable choice, moves the 2050 and 2100 medians by 5 mm or less at 1.8--3.6 $^\circ$C, so the results are insensitive to $t_{\mathrm{r}}$ between 2035 and 2040.

We obtain the GMSL forecast by integrating Eq.~\ref{eq:blend_rate} forward from the forecast origin, $t_{\mathrm{f}} = 2025$, the last year of NASA altimetry data at the time of writing. The starting level is that of the same quadratic fit at $t_{\mathrm{f}}$, relative to its level in 2000, so that 
\begin{equation}
	H_{\mathrm{forecast}}(t) = H_{\mathrm{obs}}(t_{\mathrm{f}}) + \int_{t_{\mathrm{f}}}^{t} \dot{H}_{\mathrm{forecast}}(t')\,dt'.
	\label{eq:blend_level}
\end{equation}
Each member starts from the level of its own draw of the quadratic fit at $t_{\mathrm{f}}$, so the starting level carries the 6.4~mm (1$\sigma$) uncertainty of the fit and stays consistent with that member's early rate. Taking the starting level from the fit, rather than from the altimetry record at a single time, keeps interannual variability out of the starting level. Blending rates rather than levels keeps the forecast continuous with the quadratic fit at the forecast origin, so the offset between the component-sum level and the observations does not enter the forecast. 

Each component has its own 10,000-member ensemble of cumulative contributions $H_k(t)$, with parameters drawn independently from that component's posterior or, for components using prescribed literature sensitivities rather than data calibration, from the corresponding parameter distributions. Member $i$ of every GMST-driven component follows the same GMST path. This path is the AR6 median plus $z_i$ times the growth of the AR6 standard deviation since 2024. We take that standard deviation as the half-width from the median to the 95th percentile when $z_i$ is positive, or to the 5th percentile when $z_i$ is negative, divided by 1.645. The value $z_i$ is one standard-normal draw held for the whole century and used in every scenario. Through 2024, all members follow observed GMST. After 2024, the AR6 paths are placed on the Berkeley Earth basis by one offset shared by every component and scenario, the mean difference between the AR6 SSP2-4.5 median and Berkeley Earth GMST over 2015--2024. The total level is formed sample by sample, $H_{\mathrm{comp},i}(t)=\sum_k H_{k,i}(t)$ for $i=1,\dots,10{,}000$, so parameter draws are independent across components while the within-scenario warming path is shared. 

The component rate $\dot{H}_{\mathrm{comp}}$ used in Eq.~\ref{eq:blend_rate} is obtained by finite-differencing this summed ensemble in time before blending with the observational quadratic rate. The blending is done member by member across the 10,000-member ensemble, so the correlations within the component sum, including the shared warming path, carry into the forecast. The quadratic coefficients are drawn jointly from their fitted covariance, independently of the components. 

\subsubsection{Component-level GMST dependence}
\label{s:gmst_dependence}

Four components need an extra step to link them to projected GMST. Greenland ice discharge depends on the temperature of the ocean around Greenland rather than on GMST. Greenland SMB is emulated from a regional climate model (Section~\ref{s:greenland_smb}). The EAIS sensitivity is not constrained by the observed record (Section~\ref{s:eais}), so we adopt a literature value. Terrestrial water storage is adopted from AR6.

To project the GrIS discharge model forward in time, we relate ocean temperature to Greenland regional surface temperature through a transfer function. The Greenland regional surface temperature is in turn obtained from projected GMST through an Arctic amplification factor, the ratio of Greenland regional to global warming, drawn from a normal distribution. The subsurface ocean does not follow the surface from one year to the next. Around their linear trends over 1970--2020, Greenland regional surface temperature varies from year to year with a standard deviation of 0.80\,$^\circ$C, and EN4 ocean temperature at 200--500~m with a standard deviation of 0.19\,$^\circ$C. A regression of annual ocean temperature on annual surface temperature would mostly fit this year-to-year surface variability, which the ocean does not follow, and would therefore underestimate the response of the ocean to sustained warming. We instead relate ocean temperature to a running average of past surface temperature,
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
	T_{\mathrm{ocean}}(t) &= \xi\, \overline{T}_{\mathrm{surface}}^{\mathrm{Gr}}(t) + \chi + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{go}}(t), \\
	\frac{d\overline{T}_{\mathrm{surface}}^{\mathrm{Gr}}}{dt} &= \frac{T_{\mathrm{surface}}^{\mathrm{Gr}} - \overline{T}_{\mathrm{surface}}^{\mathrm{Gr}}}{\tau_{\mathrm{go}}},
	\end{aligned}
	\label{eq:ocean_transfer}
\end{equation}
where $T_{\mathrm{surface}}^{\mathrm{Gr}}$ is the Greenland regional surface temperature anomaly, the average over the Berkeley Earth land cells that lie in Greenland \citep{Rohde2020}, $\xi$ and $\chi$ are the gain and intercept of the transfer function, $\tau_{\mathrm{go}}$ is the response time of the subsurface ocean, and $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{go}}$ is a zero-mean residual. Both temperatures are anomalies relative to the 1995--2005 baseline. We step the running average forward in annual increments from the start of the surface record in 1850. For each whole-year value of $\tau_{\mathrm{go}}$ from 1 to 20~yr, we fit $(\xi, \chi)$ by generalized least squares with first-order autoregressive residuals over the 51 years of overlap, 1970 to 2020, and weight each value by its likelihood. The data rule out response times shorter than about 2~yr but constrain longer ones only weakly. The grid ends at 20~yr, where the likelihood has fallen to 1/22 of its maximum. The fit gives $\tau_{\mathrm{go}} = 5$ [3, 16]~yr and $\xi = 0.30$ [0.16, 0.55]. At the most likely response time, 4~yr, the residual has standard deviation $\sigma_{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{go}}} = 0.15$\,$^\circ$C and lag-1 autocorrelation 0.68. In the years the EN4 record does not cover, before 1970 and from 2021 to 2024, we apply Eq.~\ref{eq:ocean_transfer} to the observed Greenland regional surface temperature. We carry the residual into those years and into the projections as first-order autoregressive noise. 

For projections, the scenario GMST trajectories are converted to Greenland regional surface temperature using an Arctic amplification factor of $\mathrm{AA} = 2.5 \pm 0.5$. The Arctic as a whole warmed nearly four times faster than the globe over 1979--2021 \citep{Rantanen2022}, but the ratio of Greenland regional to global warming in the observations we use is lower and depends on the averaging window, spanning 2.0 to 2.7 for windows that start between 1970 and 1990 and end in 2024. Greenland regional surface temperatures are used in Eq.~\ref{eq:ocean_transfer} to obtain projected $T_{\mathrm{ocean}}$, which drives the lagged discharge model (Eqs.~\ref{eq:greenland_rate} and \ref{eq:greenland_dyn}). Each of the 10,000 Monte Carlo members draws $\mathrm{AA} \sim \mathcal{N}(2.5, 0.5^2)$, a value of $\tau_{\mathrm{go}}$ from its likelihood weights, $(\xi, \chi)$ from their fitted covariance at that $\tau_{\mathrm{go}}$, one autocorrelated path of $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{go}}$, and one draw of $(\gamma, r_0, \delta)$ from the discharge posterior. These draws propagate uncertainties in Arctic amplification, the transfer function, and discharge parameters into the projections. A single draw of $(\mathrm{AA}, \tau_{\mathrm{go}}, \xi, \chi, \gamma, r_0, \delta)$ is applied to all scenarios, so that differences between scenarios isolate the effect of the emissions pathway from parameter uncertainty.

For EAIS SMB, we use the linear model of Section~\ref{s:eais} with the literature-derived accumulation sensitivity $C_T = 60 \pm 20$~Gt yr$^{-1}$ $^\circ$C$^{-1}$ of GMST \citep{Frieler2015, Ligtenberg2013}, equivalent to $b_{\mathrm{e}} = -0.17$ mm yr$^{-1}$ $^\circ$C$^{-1}$. This sensitivity reflects the Clausius--Clapeyron scaling of snowfall with warming ($C_T > 0$ corresponds to EAIS mass gain through accumulation). Data from IMBIE-3 show EAIS in approximate balance (Fig.~\ref{fig:component_fit_diagnostics}c), so we set $c_{\mathrm{e}} = 0$. The EAIS contribution is small ($-13$ to $-23$~mm at 2100 across the 1.8--3.6 $^\circ$C scenarios) relative to the total, so our GMSL projections are largely insensitive to these parameter choices.

Terrestrial water storage (TWS) projections are adopted directly from IPCC AR6 rather than modeled here. Groundwater depletion, reservoir impoundment, and changes in land hydrology are outside the scope of this framework, and the data are too sparse for a data-driven model. At each IPCC time step we fit a normal distribution to the reported 5th, 17th, 50th, 83rd, and 95th percentiles. Each of the 10,000 members takes a single quantile of these distributions for the whole century, and its path is linearly interpolated to annual resolution. TWS varies little among scenarios and its contribution is small relative to the total, so it does not materially affect the uncertainty budget.

For the hindcast and budget-closure tests we use the \citet{Frederikse2020} TWS estimate over its full record, 1900--2018, with Monte Carlo paths whose errors accumulate from 2000, and hold it at its 2018 value thereafter. The observed GMSL records used in these tests contain TWS, so the component sum must contain it too. The TWS estimate is derived from GRACE and GRACE-FO observations over 2003--2018, partitioned from the total land-mass signal. Before 2003 it is a composite of a reconstruction of climate-driven water storage and process models for reservoir impoundment and groundwater depletion rather than a measurement. Figure~S10 of the Supplementary Material shows the hindcast with TWS set to zero before 2003.

\subsubsection{West Antarctic Ice Sheet}
\label{s:wais_forecast}

West Antarctica dominates the uncertainty in sea-level projections because its major outlet glaciers, particularly Thwaites and Pine Island, lie close to a threshold at which small changes in ice geometry could trigger self-sustaining retreat \citep{Joughin2014,PattynMorlighem2020,Fricker2025}. Whether that threshold has been, or will soon be, crossed depends on glacier-specific details that remain only partly observed. We do not attempt to constrain a GMST sensitivity for WAIS. Instead, we represent WAIS as a mixture of two scenarios. In Scenario 1 (S1), mass loss continues along the observed trend. In Scenario 2 (S2), loss accelerates well beyond it.

We make our WAIS projections independent of the warming trajectory. Growing evidence indicates that WAIS mass loss is primarily sensitive to internal ice dynamics and to deep-ocean temperatures whose 21st-century warming is largely committed regardless of emissions \citep{Naughten2023,Coulon2024,Rosier2025}. In three ice-sheet models, Thwaites Glacier keeps losing mass for 150 years even when ocean melting is removed entirely, so part of its present loss is a continuing response to past forcing that no reduction in ocean forcing can reverse on centennial timescales \citep{Williams2026}. In the ISMIP6 emulator of \citet{Edwards2021}, the median WAIS contribution over 2015--2100 differs by only about 1 cm between the SSP1-2.6 and SSP5-8.5 scenarios. This independence is limited to the 21st century, because warming over this century largely sets the Antarctic contribution in the centuries that follow \citep{Lowry2021,Seroussi2024}. Some studies find emissions dependence even before 2100, with major WAIS retreat beginning this century in some simulations under 3 $^\circ$C of warming but not under 2 $^\circ$C \citep{DeConto2021}. An emulation of the ISMIP6 Antarctic ensemble extended to 2300, calibrated to the observed 2002--2021 Antarctic mass-loss rate, finds that higher emissions raise the 2100 Antarctic contribution with probability 0.89, but its posterior median for the whole ice sheet rises by only 5 mm from the 1.4 to the 2.7 $^\circ$C scenario \citep{Lin2026}. That shift is small compared with our S1 median and 90\% credible interval of 86 [$-10$, 182] mm, and part of it arises from the SMB response that we carry separately for EAIS.

In S1, mass loss continues along the observed trajectory (Fig.~\ref{fig:component_fit_diagnostics}d). Extrapolating the trend gives a median GMSL contribution of 86 mm by 2100, 1 mm above the ISMIP6 emulator median of 85 mm \citep{Edwards2021}. A 45-year observational record cannot by itself constrain how much error accumulates when it is extrapolated a further 76 years, so a credible interval built from the fitted parameters alone would understate the uncertainty in this extrapolation. We instead treat the growth in uncertainty that the ISMIP6 emulator reports beyond the last observation as an external estimate of the extrapolation error. We add it to our parameter uncertainty as a prediction error \citep{Tarantola2005}, which widens our 90\% credible interval at 2100 to a range of $-10$ to 182 mm, closely matching the ISMIP6 emulator range of $-11$ to 182 mm.

In S2, mass loss accelerates well beyond currently observed rates. We define the S2 distribution at 2100 by its 5th percentile of 85 mm, equal to the S1 median, and its 95th percentile of 1.3 m, approximately the IPCC AR6 low-confidence Antarctic Ice Sheet 95th percentile at 2100 under the 4.4 $^\circ$C scenario \citep{Fox-Kemper2021}. The distribution is right-skewed. Its logarithm follows a skew-normal distribution with shape parameter $\lambda = 3$, reflecting the expectation that instability skews outcomes toward greater loss \citep{Robel2019}. Varying $\lambda$ from 0 to 6 moves the 2100 median of the mixture with $p(\mathrm{S1}) = 10\%$ from 0.30 to 0.20 m and its 95th percentile by 0.01 m. The trajectory of WAIS mass loss through time is set by a power law in time that starts near the observed rate and accelerates toward the 2100 value. The exponent has a median of $\beta = 2$, for which the S2 95th percentile stays within the AR6 medium-confidence range for the Antarctic Ice Sheet until about 2050 and exceeds it afterward.

Emerging research on physical processes not yet represented in ice-sheet models supports the possibility of loss well above the observed trajectory. Examples include seawater intrusion beneath grounded ice \citep{Rignot2024,BradleyHewitt2024} and the evolution of subglacial water pressure \citep{Zhao2025}. Representing seawater intrusion in a calibrated model of Thwaites Glacier roughly doubles its projected contribution by 2100 \citep{Poinelli2026}. The ISMIP6 ensemble itself implies far less loss than S2. \citet{Lin2026} identify a set of ice-sheet model choices that remain consistent with the observed mass-loss rate and yield 157 [98, 254] mm for the whole ice sheet at 2100 under the 4.4 $^\circ$C scenario. The 95th percentile of that range is roughly a fifth of our S2 95th percentile for WAIS alone. That ensemble largely omits marine ice cliff instability, subglacial hydrology, and ice damage, so the difference reflects processes absent from current ice-sheet models. The S2 95th percentile adopted here should therefore not be read as an upper bound on plausible loss, and Fig.~\ref{fig:wais_median_heatmap} shows how the WAIS contribution changes with it.

The final probability density is a simple mixture of S1 and S2, weighted by the probability of S1, $p(\mathrm{S1})$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:wais_p_s1_sweep}). Its median, 95th percentile, and other quantiles are determined primarily by $p(\mathrm{S1})$ and the S2 95th percentile (Fig.~\ref{fig:wais_median_heatmap}). We hold the S2 95th percentile fixed and use $p(\mathrm{S1})$ as the control variable. We present two storylines at the limits of the IPCC terms ``very likely'' (90\%) and ``very unlikely'' (10\%): a slow-WAIS storyline with $p(\mathrm{S1}) = 90\%$, in which continued mass loss along the observed trajectory is very likely, and a fast-WAIS storyline with $p(\mathrm{S1}) = 10\%$, in which it is very unlikely.

\section{Results}

We first test the framework against observations and then present projections for each component and for total GMSL. A hindcast matters for judging a model's projections, because it tests forecasting skill against data and indicates the horizon over which the projections can be trusted.

\subsection{Validation}

We validate the model in two ways. First, we test whether the sum of the separately constrained components accounts for the observed rate of GMSL rise (budget closure). Second, we run the model over the past century with observed GMST (a hindcast) and compare it with independent GMSL records.

\subsubsection{Total GMSL budget closure}
\label{s:budget_close}

Satellite altimetry over 1993--2025 provides observations of GMSL \citep{Beckley2017,Hamlington2020} that can be compared with the sum of the components (Fig.~\ref{fig:budget_closure}). Total GMSL is withheld from calibration to serve as a validation dataset. The thermosteric, glacier, and Peninsula contributions come from the calibrated models driven by observed GMST, and Greenland discharge from its model driven by observed ocean temperature. WAIS and EAIS enter as the IMBIE-3 records \citep{Otosaka2026} through 2023, continued by the WAIS fit and the EAIS SMB model. For Greenland SMB we use the RACMO-derived SMB of \citet{Mouginot2019} through 2018 and the emulator thereafter. Terrestrial water storage is adopted from \citet{Frederikse2020} and held at its 2018 value after their record ends. The uncharted-glacier term, described in the hindcast subsection below, is zero from 2000 onward.

The component sum accounts for 89--104\% of the observed rate of GMSL rise over three epochs (1993--2025, 2000--2025, 2010--2025; Fig.~\ref{fig:budget_closure}). Because each component is constrained independently, we treat the components' rate uncertainties as independent and combine them in quadrature. Each component's individual uncertainty comes directly from its Monte Carlo ensemble. For a given epoch, we fit a linear trend to every ensemble member and take the standard deviation of the resulting trends. We repeat this for each epoch, so the combined uncertainty differs from one epoch to the next. We add the uncertainty of the altimetry trend over the same epoch in quadrature, combining the published altimetry error covariance, the serial correlation of the residuals, and the uncertainty of the glacial isostatic adjustment correction. Errors on the cumulative records that enter the closure directly (IMBIE-3 for WAIS and EAIS, the observed Greenland SMB, and terrestrial water storage) are drawn as correlated annual increments. By this measure, the residual between the observed and summed rates is 0.47$\sigma$, 0.36$\sigma$, and 0.99$\sigma$ of the combined uncertainty for 1993--2025, 2000--2025, and 2010--2025.

The largest shortfall is over 2010--2025 and arises after 2018. The component sum accounts for about 102\% of the observed rate over 2010--2018 and about 77\% over 2018--2025, when the closure uses the SMB emulator in place of the observed Greenland SMB and holds terrestrial water storage at its 2018 value.

\centering

\subsubsection{Hindcasting the level, rate, and transient sensitivity}

We validate the combined GMST-sensitive model by two hindcasting approaches. In both, the GMST-dependent components are forced with observed GMST back to 1900 \citep{Rohde2020}, with an emphasis on the period after 1950, when increasingly reliable GMSL data are available. WAIS follows the IMBIE-3 record, and its rate is set to zero through 1979. Terrestrial water storage follows \citet{Frederikse2020} over 1900--2018. The glacier hindcast also includes glaciers that are missing from the inventories on which the GlaMBIE record is built, because they are too small to map or because they melted away after 1901. \citet{Parkes2018} estimate that these uncharted glaciers contributed 0.17 to 0.53 mm yr$^{-1}$ over 1901--1990, most of it early in the century, and 0.08 to 0.21 mm yr$^{-1}$ over 1993--2010. We add this contribution as a prescribed series, sampled between their lower and upper bounds. For each bound, the rate declines linearly over 1901--1990, with a mean equal to their 1901--1990 value, and then holds their 1993--2010 rate. It does not depend on GMST and is set to zero from 2000, when the GlaMBIE record that calibrates the glacier model begins.

The first comparison is between the GMSL level and rate from SPECTRUM and those of four records. These are the \citet{Frederikse2020} and \citet{Dangendorf2024} reconstructions, the IPCC AR6 compilation \citep{Fox-Kemper2021}, and satellite altimetry \citep{Beckley2017}. The AR6 compilation includes \citet{Frederikse2020} and an earlier version of the \citet{Dangendorf2024} reconstruction. For this comparison, we use the same rate-space blending scheme as the forecast (Section~\ref{s:forecasting_model}), reflected about the forecast origin so that the satellite-era quadratic dominates at late times and the component models at early times. The midpoint of the blend falls at 2015, the mirror of 2035 about $t_{\mathrm{f}} = 2025$, with $\tau_{\mathrm{r}} = 5$ yr unchanged. The observational term thus carries the same weight at 2025 as in the forecast and falls to 0.05 by 2000, so the hindcast over the reconstruction era tests the component models rather than restating the altimetry extrapolation. For the second comparison, we calculate the transient sensitivity of the SPECTRUM output, the change in the rate of GMSL rise per degree of warming, $\alpha = d\dot{H}/dT$, where $\dot{H}$ is the rate of GMSL rise.

The agreement between the hindcast and the GMSL records differs among periods (Fig.~\ref{fig:observations}a). Over the satellite era we compare rates over the window that each record covers, using the component sum without blending, because over this period the blend includes the satellite-era quadratic. The hindcast rate is 3.32 mm yr$^{-1}$ compared with 3.35 mm yr$^{-1}$ for the \citet{Frederikse2020} reconstruction (1993--2018), 3.36 compared with 3.17 mm yr$^{-1}$ for the \citet{Dangendorf2024} reconstruction (1993--2021), 3.35 compared with 3.23 mm yr$^{-1}$ for the IPCC AR6 observational compilation (1993--2020; \citealt{Fox-Kemper2021}), and 3.40 compared with 3.29 mm yr$^{-1}$ for satellite altimetry (1993--2025; \citealt{Beckley2017}). 

Over 1950--2000 the hindcast rate is 0.75 mm yr$^{-1}$ compared with 1.02, 1.01, and 1.28 mm yr$^{-1}$ for the \citet{Frederikse2020} and \citet{Dangendorf2024} reconstructions and the IPCC AR6 compilation, and the hindcast level at 1950 lies 10 to 23 mm above them. Three terms in the hindcast bear on this difference. Of the hindcast rate, 0.21 mm yr$^{-1}$ comes from the prescribed uncharted-glacier term. The hindcast includes the terrestrial water storage of \citet{Frederikse2020}, which lowered GMSL by 18 mm from 1950 to 2000. Before 2003 this estimate combines a reservoir inventory with hydrological and groundwater models rather than observations. With terrestrial water storage set to zero before 2003, the 1950--2000 rate of the summed components would be 1.18 mm yr$^{-1}$ (Fig.~S10 of the Supplementary Material). In the budget of \citet{Frederikse2020}, Greenland raised GMSL from 1950 to 2000 by 8 mm more than in our hindcast, of which about 3 mm comes from the Greenland peripheral glaciers that \citet{Frederikse2020} count with Greenland and we count with glaciers. Before 1972, their Greenland contribution comes only from \citet{Kjeldsen2015}, which resolves a single mean rate of mass loss over 1900--1983. 

For years before 1950, covered only by the \citet{Frederikse2020} and \citet{Dangendorf2024} reconstructions, the divergence grows. The hindcast rate is 1.14 mm yr$^{-1}$ compared with 1.39 and 1.64 mm yr$^{-1}$ over 1900--1950, and the hindcast level at 1900 is 31--37 mm above the reconstructions, relative to the 2000 baseline, although the median line from SPECTRUM stays within the 90\% interval of \citet{Dangendorf2024}. This pre-1950 divergence follows from a design choice. We include only model representations that can be fitted to observations and that describe the ongoing response to warming. Representing GMSL in earlier, colder periods would require more complete land-ice process models, which are outside our scope. We therefore treat the pre-1950 hindcast as qualitatively informative but do not draw quantitative conclusions from it. We restrict our validation claims to the 75-year, post-1950 period.

Figure~\ref{fig:observations}b compares the rate of GMSL rise of our component model with observed rates as a function of GMST, with both rates estimated by the same local regression using a tricube kernel with a 30-yr half-width. The color of the model line shows its transient sensitivity along the 1957--2025 trajectory. The model's transient sensitivity follows the observed increase in the rate of rise with warming. The slope of the observed rate as a function of GMST agrees with the sensitivity of 3.4 mm yr$^{-1}$ $^\circ$C$^{-1}$ found by \citet{Rahmstorf2007} nearly 20 years ago.

Together, the level, rate, and transient-sensitivity comparisons test our framework over a 75-year window (1950--2025) spanning about 1.0--1.1 $^\circ$C of GMST change (11-yr means), with observations of GMSL itself withheld from calibration. Over the satellite era its rates are 3.32 to 3.40 mm yr$^{-1}$, compared with 3.17 to 3.35 mm yr$^{-1}$ in the four observational records over matched windows. Its 1950--2000 rate is lower than those of the three longer records by about the size of the effect of the model-based terrestrial water storage. With that term set to zero before 2003, the hindcast 1950--2000 rate rises by 0.43 mm yr$^{-1}$ to 1.18 mm yr$^{-1}$, within the range of the three records. The 1950--2000 comparison therefore depends on a term that observations do not constrain before 2003. 

This validation window is comparable in duration to the remaining forecast horizon and in warming to the 2.7 $^\circ$C scenario, which adds 1.4 $^\circ$C between 2025 and 2100. The 1.8 $^\circ$C scenario adds 0.4 $^\circ$C, less than the warming across the validation window, although it takes GMST above any observed value. The 3.6 $^\circ$C scenario adds 2.6 $^\circ$C, so its forecast extends furthest beyond what the hindcast tests. This validation does not, however, extend to processes without comparable historical constraints. WAIS, the largest source of forecast uncertainty, is represented in this work by scenario-based storylines rather than a data-driven model (Section~\ref{s:wais_forecast}).

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Data-driven forecasts as part of a hierarchy}

The SPECTRUM framework occupies an intermediate level of a hierarchy of sea-level models ordered by complexity, so its results can be compared with both simpler and more complex models. The simplest models extrapolate the observed trends. Extending our quadratic fit to the satellite altimetry record of GMSL to 2100, following \citet{Nerem2022} and \citet{Hamlington2024}, who stop at 2050, gives 0.63 [0.40, 0.86] m of rise relative to 2000. At the local scale, trends and accelerations at 222 tide gauges over 1970--2023, extrapolated to 2050, are consistent with the AR6 medium-confidence projections within the likely range at 96\% of locations \citep{Wang2025GRL}.

Next in complexity are the semi-empirical models, which relate the rate of global mean sea-level rise to GMST. Applying the semi-empirical relation of \citet{Rahmstorf2007} to the 2.7 $^\circ$C scenario gives 0.73 [0.67, 0.82] m relative to 2000, where the interval reflects only the spread of warming paths. Our slow-WAIS forecast ($p(\mathrm{S1}) = 90\%$) gives 0.54 [0.42, 0.73] m at 2.7 $^\circ$C (Fig.~\ref{fig:ridge_projections}a). Its median is 92 mm less than the extrapolation of GMSL and 0.19 m less than the semi-empirical model.

At the complex end of the hierarchy are the process-model assessments of IPCC AR6 \citep{Fox-Kemper2021}, which report two sets of projections. The medium-confidence set is built from processes that can be modeled with confidence, while the low-confidence set admits ice-sheet processes such as marine ice cliff instability along with expert judgment. In SPECTRUM, lowering $p(\mathrm{S1})$ moves the forecast from the medium-confidence set toward the low-confidence set. To compare with AR6, we shift our forecast to the AR6 1995--2014 baseline, which lowers each value by 13 mm. On that baseline, our slow-WAIS forecast gives 0.44 [0.33, 0.62] m at 1.8 $^\circ$C, 0.53 [0.41, 0.72] m at 2.7 $^\circ$C, and 0.63 [0.49, 0.83] m at 3.6 $^\circ$C. The AR6 medium-confidence medians are 0.44, 0.56, and 0.68 m. Our slow-WAIS median equals the AR6 median at 1.8 $^\circ$C and is 5 and 8\% lower at 2.7 and 3.6 $^\circ$C, whereas the fast-WAIS medians are 15 to 33\% higher. Our slow-WAIS 95th percentiles are lower than the AR6 medium-confidence 95th percentiles at all three warming levels (0.62, 0.72, and 0.83 m, compared with 0.78, 0.95, and 1.11 m).

At all three warming levels, the difference between our fast-WAIS forecast and the AR6 medium-confidence projections comes from the weight given to rapid WAIS mass loss. The thermosteric, glacier, and Greenland contributions together differ from their AR6 medians by $+18$, $-4$, and $-26$ mm at 1.8, 2.7, and 3.6 $^\circ$C, compared with differences of 145, 120, and 98 mm in the fast-WAIS total.

Our fast-WAIS forecast is higher than the AR6 low-confidence projections at 1.8 and 2.7 $^\circ$C and lower at 4.4 $^\circ$C, the three scenarios for which AR6 provides them. At 2.7 $^\circ$C, our median of 0.68 m and 95th percentile of 1.67 m are greater than the low-confidence values of 0.57 and 1.18 m. At 4.4 $^\circ$C, our median of 0.86 m and 95th percentile of 1.84 m are less than the low-confidence values of 0.88 and 2.28 m.

\subsection{Completeness of the uncertainty budget}

Our intervals include most sources of uncertainty, but not alternative model forms for the calibrated components. \citet{Aschwanden2021} set aside the emissions scenario and separate the remaining uncertainty in an ice-sheet forecast into four parts. These are the model structure, the initial state, the model parameters, and random variability in the forcing. They argue that the ISMIP6 ensemble sampled model structure but not parameters, and that for Greenland the parametric spread could be the larger of the two. SPECTRUM samples the parameters of every calibrated component. WAIS, the one component whose uncertainty is dominated by model structure, is represented instead by a mixture of scenarios. Each warming scenario allows a range of GMST paths, and each ensemble member follows one path shared by all components. Errors in the initial state and year-to-year variability are absorbed into the calibrated error models rather than represented separately. The omission of alternative model forms matters most under the slow-WAIS storyline, in which the thermosteric, glacier, and Greenland contributions account for 39 to 49\% of the summed widths of the 90\% credible intervals of the four largest components. Under the fast-WAIS storyline it matters little, because WAIS accounts for more than 90\% of the variance. 

We tested one alternative functional form directly. Our glacier model assumes that glaciers never shrink toward a size in balance with the warmer climate, which is the limit of an infinitely long response time (Eq.~\ref{eq:glacier_balance}). Glaciers do shrink over decades to centuries, and their loss rate declines as they shrink \citep{Roe2021}. A model that includes this adjustment fits the glacier record as well as ours when the response time is a century or more. Averaged over response times of 20 to 200 years, it gives about 30 mm less glacier sea-level rise from 2000 to 2100 at each warming level, and 11 to 13 mm less if response times up to millennia are equally plausible. Our glacier projections lie within the range of the process model of \citet{Rounce2023}, so the bias from neglecting this adjustment is likely nearer the smaller of the two estimates. 

Three further tests vary inputs to the Greenland discharge model. Moving the center of the sampled Arctic amplification factor from 2.5 to either end of the observed range of the Greenland-to-global warming ratio (2.0 and 2.7) moves the 2100 median by 4.9 mm or less. Shifting the 200--500~m ocean-forcing layer 100~m shallower or deeper moves it by 2.5 mm or less, and extending the range of ocean response times $\tau_{\mathrm{go}}$ from 1--20~yr to 1--40~yr moves it by 2.1 mm or less. No test changes the width of the 90\% credible interval by more than 2.3 mm.

We do not calculate the integral over model structure itself, in which a projection is averaged over a distribution across model structures \citep{Aschwanden2021}. Assigning weights that sum to one to the models that have been built would treat each model as an independent sample of the ways the system might be represented. In fact, those models describe the same system, rely on many of the same assumptions, and are tested against many of the same observations. The ISMIP6 emulator of \citet{Edwards2021}, which underlies the AR6 medium-confidence Antarctic projections, is an example of combining the models that have been built. It treats each multi-model ensemble as the output of a single stochastic simulator and uses all models, because observations cannot yet identify which responds most realistically. A random-effects test by its authors found that dependence among the models has little effect on the projections at 2100. Our two WAIS storylines bracket the outcome rather than estimate a distribution over model structures.

For components calibrated to observations, SPECTRUM implements the conditioning step of \citet{Aschwanden2021}, in which a prior over projections is filtered by observations. Each calibrated component uses its own observational record, and observed GMSL is withheld from all calibrations. The hindcast therefore asks whether the separately fitted components add up to the observed total.

\citet{Lin2026} take an alternative approach for Antarctica. They emulate the ISMIP6-2300 ensemble, whose ice-model choices were documented but not sampled systematically, and place a uniform prior over 18 ice-dynamical features and the choice of climate model. In the raw ISMIP6 simulations emulated by \citet{Lin2026}, choices made within the ice-sheet models account for 64\% of the 2015--2100 variance, about four times the share of the forcing. In their drift-corrected simulations the ranking reverses, with 23\% from the model choices compared with 48\% from the forcing. They then update the probabilities with the observed mass-loss rate. The observations constrain the sliding law, but for most other choices the result remains strongly dependent on the prior. A two-decade record says little about processes that barely affect the recent trend but strongly affect the century-scale response. \citet{Lin2026} describe this remaining dependence on the prior as strong ambiguity and bound it with probability boxes, which give the range of probabilities across a set of plausible priors. The same difficulty leads us to represent WAIS with two storylines rather than a single calibrated distribution.

\subsection{Two dimensions of sea-level risk}

Sea-level risk in 2100 has two dimensions. The first comes from the thermosteric, glacier, and Greenland contributions, which respond to warming and can be reduced by cutting emissions. Their warming sensitivities are calibrated with observations or, for Greenland SMB, emulated from a regional climate model, and we validate the fits with decades of observations withheld from calibration. The collective contribution of these components shifts systematically across warming trajectories and can be reduced by emissions cuts (Fig.~\ref{fig:components}a). Under the slow-WAIS storyline, the 90\% credible interval of the forecast spans 0.29 m at 1.8 $^\circ$C, 0.31 m at 2.7 $^\circ$C, and 0.34 m at 3.6 $^\circ$C. If WAIS loses mass slowly, emissions policy is the main lever for managing sea-level risk. Under the slow-WAIS storyline, warming of 3.6 $^\circ$C rather than 1.8 $^\circ$C adds 0.19 m to the median rise by 2100. That added rise increases coastal adaptation costs by US\$25 billion per year (scaled from \citet{Tiggeloven2020}; Supplementary Material).

The second dimension, set by WAIS, is the width of the upper tail, which changes little with emissions over the 21st century. The fast-WAIS intervals, which admit rapid, feedback-driven WAIS mass loss, are 3.5 to 4.0 times wider than the slow-WAIS intervals (Fig.~\ref{fig:ridge_projections}). Under the fast-WAIS storyline, WAIS accounts for more than 90\% of the forecast variance, and in our forecast it does not depend on the emissions pathway before 2100. The tail of the forecast distribution holds the outcomes with the largest human and economic consequences. It is controlled almost entirely by whether WAIS follows modern loss trends or transitions to more rapid retreat \citep{Turney2020, Dutton2015, Graham2022}. Under the fast-WAIS storyline, emissions reductions shift the whole distribution downward and lower the median and the 95th percentile by similar amounts. They do not narrow the upper tail, whose spread above the median (0.99 m at both 1.8 and 3.6 $^\circ$C) is set by WAIS. The probability of exceeding 2 m relative to pre-industrial is 3\% at 1.8 $^\circ$C and 4\% at 3.6 $^\circ$C. Even at 1.8 $^\circ$C, emissions reductions alone therefore cannot remove the high-risk outcomes. The 3\% probability at 1.8 $^\circ$C rests on $p(\mathrm{S1}) = 10\%$ and on an S2 95th percentile set near the AR6 low-confidence Antarctic value, which combines structured expert judgment with simulations that include marine ice cliff instability. Emerging research on processes absent from current ice-sheet models points toward mechanisms that could raise those values (Section~\ref{s:wais_forecast}).


Costs to coastal communities illustrate the gap between the fast-WAIS and slow-WAIS storylines (Fig.~\ref{fig:ridge_projections}). Risk-averse planning for long-lived infrastructure needs information on the upper tail of the distribution \citep{vandeWal2022}. Here we use the 95th percentile as a design threshold (Fig.~\ref{fig:ridge_projections}b). Under the current-policy warming trajectory (2.7 $^\circ$C), this threshold is 0.95 m higher by 2100 under the fast-WAIS storyline than under the slow-WAIS storyline (1.68 m compared with 0.73 m). This difference translates into US\$128 billion per year in coastal adaptation costs, scaled from \citet{Tiggeloven2020}, and US\$12.5 trillion per year in coastal flood damages without further adaptation, interpolated linearly between the values of \citet{Jevrejeva2018} (Supplementary Material). For context, the fast-WAIS 95th percentile implies more than seven times the sea-level rise of the past 125 years within the next 75 years, and the slow-WAIS 95th percentile about three times.
% These numbers reflect global population densities, with the highest densities near coastlines, and the fact that the locations of coastal communities and infrastructure are calibrated to lower sea levels.


Scientific research is necessary to provide actionable information for coastal adaptation. Further research may add to the evidence for rapid WAIS mass loss rather than build confidence in slow loss, because key processes remain missing from or crudely represented in models \citep{Fricker2025,Millman2026}. Under the fast-WAIS storyline at 2.7 $^\circ$C, there is a 5\% probability of less than 0.50 m and a 5\% probability of more than 1.68 m of GMSL rise by 2100. While the range of outcomes remains this wide, decisions on coastal planning, adaptation, and managed retreat that affect millions of lives will be made under large uncertainty. The scale of this uncertainty and its consequences, along with the prospect of committed sea-level rise that emissions cuts cannot reduce, has motivated research into whether targeted interventions could slow ice loss from glaciers and ice sheets \citep{Wolovick2018,MacAyeal2024}, for example by removing water from the glacier bed \citep{Meyer2026} or cooling the bed with thermosyphons \citep{Miller2026}. Others have argued that such interventions would be infeasible and environmentally damaging \citep{Siegert2025}. Scientific research is also necessary to determine how much committed sea-level rise, and the uncertainty in it, can be reduced.


\section{Conclusions}

Sea-level rise this century has two parts that call for different responses. The first is the combined contribution of ocean thermal expansion and melting of glaciers and the Greenland Ice Sheet. These components' sensitivities to warming are calibrated with observations or emulated from a regional climate model, and emissions cuts reduce each of these contributions. The second is WAIS. Observations cannot yet tell whether its mass loss will accelerate, and it dominates the uncertainty in the forecast.

SPECTRUM uses a simple physical model for each contributor that the record can constrain, including a new ocean-forced model of Greenland outlet-glacier discharge. It represents WAIS with two scenarios, S1 and S2, of which S1 extends the observed trend. With observed GMSL withheld from every calibration, the summed components account for 89--104\% of the satellite-altimetry rate over three epochs since 1993. Over 1950--2000, their rate is 0.3--0.5~mm\,yr$^{-1}$ lower than observed. Without the model-based terrestrial water storage before 2003, which observations do not constrain, this rate lies within the range of the observed records. The 1950--2025 validation period spans about 1.0--1.1 $^\circ$C of warming. The 2.7 $^\circ$C (SSP2-4.5) scenario adds a comparable 1.4 $^\circ$C after 2025, and the 3.6 $^\circ$C (SSP3-7.0) scenario adds 2.6 $^\circ$C, more than twice as much.

Under the slow-WAIS storyline ($p(\mathrm{S1}) = 90\%$), median GMSL rise over 2000--2100 is 0.45, 0.54, and 0.64 m for the 1.8, 2.7, and 3.6 $^\circ$C scenarios, with a 90\% credible interval of 0.42 to 0.73 m at 2.7 $^\circ$C. Under the fast-WAIS storyline ($p(\mathrm{S1}) = 10\%$), the median at 2.7 $^\circ$C is 0.69 m, with a 90\% credible interval of 0.50 to 1.68 m. The slow-WAIS median at 2.7 $^\circ$C is 92 mm below the extrapolated satellite-era trend and 0.19 m below a semi-empirical model. On the AR6 baseline, the slow-WAIS medians match the AR6 medium-confidence medians or fall below them by up to 8\%.

Even under the slow-WAIS storyline, which assigns slow loss 90\% probability, WAIS accounts for 89 to 96\% of the forecast variance at 2100, mostly through the possibility of rapid loss. Under the same storyline, however, the thermosteric, glacier, and Greenland contributions set 39 to 49\% of the summed widths of the component 90\% credible intervals. The intervals of these three contributions do not include alternative model forms.

Emissions cuts lower the median rise. Under the slow-WAIS storyline, holding warming to 1.8 $^\circ$C rather than 3.6 $^\circ$C lowers the median by 0.19 m. We assume, however, that WAIS mass loss before 2100 is largely committed and independent of warming \citep{Naughten2023,Coulon2024,Rosier2025,Williams2026}. Under the fast-WAIS storyline, the same cut therefore leaves the 95th percentile about 1 m above the median, and the probability of exceeding 2 m relative to pre-industrial by 2100 falls from 4 to 3\%. These tail values rest on $p(\mathrm{S1})$ and on the S2 95th percentile. Beyond 2100, emissions matter for WAIS as well, because 21st-century warming largely sets the long-term Antarctic commitment \citep{Lowry2021,Seroussi2024}.

Better observations and models could tell planners which WAIS outcome they face, although such work may add to the evidence for rapid loss rather than rule it out \citep{Fricker2025}. Over this century, emissions cuts shift the distribution of sea-level rise, and the rate of WAIS mass loss sets its upper tail.
