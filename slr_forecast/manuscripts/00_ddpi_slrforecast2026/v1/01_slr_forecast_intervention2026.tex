\begin{abstract}

Sea-level Projections from Empirical Calibration of Thermodynamical, Reduced-physics Models (SPECTRUM)

Every centimeter of sea-level rise exposes millions more people to annual coastal flooding, yet projections of sea-level come with large uncertainties. Many of the commonly used projections rely on complex models calibrated on data too sparse to adequately constrain them, causing the outputs to depend on model assumptions in ways that remain unquantified and nontransparent. Here, we construct a data-driven sea-level forecast by calibrating parsimonious physical models for individual sea-level contributors against decades-long independent observational records. When forced with observed surface temperatures, the calibrated components sum to match observed global mean sea level rise (GMSLR) from satellite altimetry collected since 1993. Hindcasts match GMSLR reconstructions within error to at least 1950, or approximately 1 $\circ$C warming. Our forecasts show a 53\% probability of GMSLR exceeding 1 meter, and 4.5\% of exceeding 2 meters, by the end of the century. The dynamics of West Antarctic glaciers account for $\sim90$\% of the forecast uncertainty and reveal orthogonal dimensions of sea-level risk: emissions reduction decreases median projections related to the predictable, temperature-sensitive components, but leaves the high-risk tail of the distribution largely unchanged, exposing hundreds of millions of people to accelerating sea-level risks.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Every centimeter of sea-level rise exposes roughly 1.6 million more people to annual coastal
flooding \citep{Kulp2019}, and without further adaptation annual flood damages reach
US\$14--18 trillion per year at 0.9--1.1~m of rise \citep{Jevrejeva2018}. Planning and adaptation for these risks depends on how skillfully we can predict the rate of sea-level rise. Forecasts published over four decades span a wide range of methods and outcomes, and the high-end estimates published since the Sixth Assessment Report of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (AR6) span 0.6--2.0~m of rise by 2100 \citep{Garner2026}. Coastal planning leans on AR6 itself, which projects 0.4--0.7~m this century, but with deep uncertainty attributed to ice sheet processes not well represented in models \citep{Fox-Kemper2021,Hirschfeld2023}. The gaps in our understanding of ice sheet processes present significant challenges as continued warming ensures that projections of sea-level rise will remain out of sample with respect to the modern observational record no matter the projection method. The central difficulty is therefore not building a model but calibrating it, testing it against data withheld from calibration, and reporting uncertainties in a form that separates the various sources. A recent community synthesis makes this case, calling for projections narrowed by observations and for uncertainty characterized by its structure and not only by its magnitude \citep{Felikson2025}.

%Structural assumptions carry comparable weight. The stress exponent of the flow law for ice is usually assigned a value lower than observations of fast-flowing ice shelves indicate \citep{Millstein2022}, and where grounding lines retreat rapidly, uncertainty in that exponent produces a wider spread in projected ice loss than uncertainty in climate forcing does \citep{GetraerMorlighem2025}. Calibration can hide such a bias, because a model that assumes $n = 3$ can be initialized to match observations of an $n = 4$ glacier yet underestimate its 100-year sea-level contribution by 21 to 35\% in retreat benchmarks \citep{Martin2026}. Two ice-sheet models calibrated against different observations of the same glacier give mid-century loss rates differing by more than a factor of two \citep{Goldberg2026}. %Forecasting research across many fields finds that models carrying more free parameters than their data can constrain forecast worse than simpler ones \citep{Green2015,Makridakis2020}.

Sea-level projection models span a wide range of complexity, and their strengths are complementary. Process-based ice-sheet models are the most complex. They solve the equations of ice flow, deformation, and fracture, and are where competing hypotheses about mechanics of ice loss can be posed and tested \citep{Schoof2007,Morlighem2024}. Their parameters, such as the basal sliding coefficient, are inferred from observations by ill-posed inverse problems in which the data constrain only a limited set of parameter directions \citep{Isaac2015,Petra2014}. They depart from reconstructions of historical GMSLR in hindcast \citep{Aschwanden2021}, and most projections initialized in 2016 grow the Antarctic Ice Sheet through the years that overlap the satellite record, with outcomes that remain sensitive to the state a model starts from \citep{Fricker2025}. At the other extreme of model complexity, extrapolations of the observed record and semi-empirical fits of the rate of GMSLR to global mean surface temperature (GMST) are transparent and anchored in the quantity being predicted \citep{Nerem2022,Rahmstorf2007,Vermeer2009,Kopp2016}. They track the observed record at least as well as contemporaneous process-based projections \citep{Rahmstorf2012}. But they sacrifice insight into which processes are responsible for the observed trends, and the fitted relationship need not hold as individual contributors respond on different timescales \citep{Church2013}. Between the extremes in model complexity sit component-level models, which separate GMSLR into its physical contributors and calibrate each one against observations, model output, or expert assessment \citep{Kopp2014,Mengel2016,Wong2017, Perrette2013,Nauels2017,Kopp2023}, trading spatial detail for parsimony and a direct link to data.

No one class of model can be at once the most physically detailed, the most transparent, and the most tightly constrained by data, so treating any one of them as authoritative discards what the others offer. Ordered by complexity they instead form a hierarchy, a practice long leveraged elsewhere in climate science, where ordering models from idealized to comprehensive has served understanding better than process-based models alone \citep{Held2005,Jeevanjee2017,Maher2019}. Each level in the hierarchy can be calibrated and tested against data before the next is trusted, giving observations the formal role in refining projections that recent assessments of ice-sheet and sea-level projection have called for \citep{Aschwanden2021,Felikson2025}. An extrapolation of the observed record sets a scenario-independent reference that any more elaborate model should be able to reproduce. A semi-empirical fit adds a dependence on warming and little else, which isolates that sensitivity and makes it testable on its own. A component-level model then can show whether the same sensitivity can be recovered from the separate physics of the ocean, the glaciers, and the ice sheets, each calibrated against its own observational record. A process-based model can then be used as a diagnostic to highlight which mechanisms within those contributors are responsible. Calibration and validation are then carried out within the space of models rather than against a single preferred one, and agreement between levels becomes a diagnostic in its own right. Where the levels agree, a result rests on more than one set of assumptions, and agreement of that kind carries more weight than agreement among models that share their assumptions \citep{Lipscomb2025}. Where they diverge, the divergence localizes what the data do not yet constrain and can be used to identify the observations that would provide further constraints. The simplest levels often carry the most transferable insight, because what they assume and include is small enough to state clearly, to test directly, and to be understood by those who use the model outputs \citep{Green2015,Makridakis2020}. A hierarchy built this way complements process-based modeling rather than substituting for it. %A numerical model of a natural system can be tested against data and against other models, but it cannot be shown to be correct \citep{Oreskes1994}. 

Here we construct a data-driven, component-level forecast of 21st-century sea-level rise. Where the observational record can constrain a contributor to GMSLR, we give it a parsimonious physical model calibrated by Bayesian inference against an independent record spanning decades, among them NOAA thermosteric estimates \citep{Levitus2012}, EN4 subsurface ocean temperature \citep{Good2013}, Greenland outlet-glacier discharge \citep{Mouginot2019}, GlaMBIE \citep{GlaMBIE2025}, and IMBIE-3 \citep{Otosaka2026}. The remaining contributors, among them Greenland and East Antarctic surface mass balance and terrestrial water storage, are adopted from published model sensitivities and assessments \citep{Fettweis2020,Noel2021,Frieler2015,Frederikse2020}. We call the framework SPECTRUM, for Sea-level Projections from Empirical Calibration of Thermodynamical, Reduced-physics Models. Unless otherwise noted, all sea-level values in the main text are global-mean 21st-century contributions referenced to the year 2000. Throughout, emission scenarios are labeled by their approximate median end-of-century global mean surface temperature rounded to the nearest integer and referenced to the 1850-1900 (pre-industrial) mean: 2$\,^\circ$C (SSP1-2.6), 3$\,^\circ$C (SSP2-4.5), and 4$\,^\circ$C (SSP3-7.0). %Some of its component models are adapted from the scholarly literature, and for those our contribution lies in the calibration data, the validation, and the treatment of uncertainty. The exception is Greenland Ice Sheet discharge, for which we develop a three-parameter model driven by the temperature of the surrounding ocean. West Antarctic discharge is the one contributor we do not fit to warming, because the observational record cannot yet distinguish a self-sustaining retreat from one sustained by ocean melting from below \citep{Hill2023,Reese2023,Sergienko2026}. We represent WAIS mass loss as a structured mixture of scenarios. Observed GMSLR and its sensitivity to observed GMST are withheld from every component calibration and reserved as diagnostics for the summed model, so the central test of the framework is out of sample. The independently calibrated components close the satellite altimetry budget \citep{Beckley2017,Hamlington2020} and hindcast the level, the rate, and the transient sensitivity of GMSLR since the early 20th century and across a little more than 1 $^\circ$C of warming \citep{Frederikse2020,Fox-Kemper2021}, a record none of them saw.

\subsection{Component models, calibration, and validation}
\label{s:component_model_derivation}

\subsubsection{Greenland Ice Sheet}

The Greenland Ice Sheet (GrIS) is the largest land-ice contributor and has lost mass every year since the 1990s \citep{Mouginot2019,Mankoff2021}. That loss is the imbalance between solid-ice discharge and surface mass balance (SMB), where SMB is the difference between mass gained by snow accumulation and mass lost to melting. 

GrIS discharge is sensitive to ocean temperature and surface melt runoff \citep{Straneo2013,Slater2020,Wood2021}. Warm, subsurface waters thin and undercut the calving fronts of marine-terminating glaciers, driving increased calving and net terminus retreat. This effect is amplified where surface runoff drives mixing and buoyant upwelling along the calving face \citep{Slater2022}. Surface melt also lubricates glacier beds, but that effect is generally short-lived and localized as the subglacial hydrological system evolves to accommodate changes in meltwater flux \citep{Schoof2010,Tedstone2013,Ultee2022}.
 
Focusing on the strong influence of ocean-induced melting on discharge, we approximate the GrIS-wide discharge as
\begin{equation}
	D = \gamma\,T_{\mathrm{ocean}}(t - \delta) + r_0,
	\label{eq:greenland_rate}
\end{equation}
where $D$ is the GrIS discharge in sea-level equivalent units, $\gamma$ is an effective discharge sensitivity to ocean temperature, $\delta$ is the lag between distal ocean warming and ice sheet response, $r_0$ is the background discharge rate at the baseline ocean temperature, and $T_{\mathrm{ocean}}$ is the ocean temperature anomaly in the ocean waters around Greenland relative to the 1995--2005 mean. Because $D$ is the gross discharge flux rather than an anomaly, it becomes a contribution to GMSL only when combined with SMB, whose accumulation term offsets most of the background rate $r_0$. We calibrate against total GrIS discharge, integrated across the 219 marine-terminating outlet glaciers resolved in the observational record \citep{Mouginot2019} to trade spatial resolution for signal-to-noise in the data. Our goal is to capture ice-sheet scale discharge rather than the individual basins and glaciers. 

The calibration is carried out on the time-integrated form of Eq.~\ref{eq:greenland_rate}, which gives the
cumulative contribution of GrIS discharge to GMSLR,
	\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:greenland_dyn}
      H_{\mathrm{D}}(t) = \int_{t_0}^{t} D dt',
	\end{equation}
with $\gamma$ and $r_0$ set constant in time. Fitting the time-integrated (cumulative) record further improves the signal-to-noise ratio, because the cumulative signal grows linearly in time while the annual errors accumulate only as its square root. It also directly constrains the total mass lost, the quantity of interest in this study.

The calibration uses 47 years of glacier discharge data \citep{Mouginot2019} and ocean temperatures from EN4 (v4.2.2, g10 bias correction) \citep{Good2013}, averaged over 200--500\,m depth, the range most relevant to Greenland's tidewater grounding lines \citep{Slater2020}, in the seas surrounding Greenland south of 80$^\circ$N (58--80$^\circ$N, 75--5$^\circ$W). This geographic limit is set by the sparse subsurface sampling farther north (Fig. \ref{fig:greenland_delay_fit}). Because the data are resolved annually, the lag $\delta$ is in integer-year values. We then fit $(\gamma, r_0)$ by weighted least squares separately for a range of $\delta$ values, using the demeaned cumulative record. Each fit carries a penalty on the mismatch between the modeled discharge rate at the end of the time-series and the rate observed over the final decade, which keeps the calibration consistent with the present-day discharge rate. The posterior is then formed as a Gaussian mixture of the fits for each $\delta$, with weights derived from the Bayesian Information Criterion. This procedure places 99.5\% of the weight on $\delta = 5$~yr and gives posterior medians $\gamma = 0.365$~mm SLE yr$^{-1}\,^\circ$C$^{-1}$ (0.312--0.419~mm\,SLE\,yr$^{-1}\,^\circ$C$^{-1}$) and $r_0 = 1.383$~mm\,SLE\,yr$^{-1}$ (1.367--1.399~mm\,SLE\,yr$^{-1}$). The calibration target is a cumulative record, resulting in correlated errors, so we report parameter intervals from a covariance that accounts for that correlation and for the excess scatter of the residuals relative to the reported observational errors, such that reduced chi-square $\chi^2/\mathrm{dof} = 1.8$, where $\chi^2$ is the error-weighted sum of squared residuals and $\mathrm{dof} = 45$ is the number of annual observations (47) less the two fitted parameters. We validate the model against a discharge time-series spanning 1986--2023 that was withheld from calibration \citep{Mankoff2021} and the time-series from the Ice Sheet Mass Balance Intercomparison Exercise (IMBIE-3) spanning 1972--2023 \citep{Otosaka2026}. The validation with \citet{Mankoff2021} is informative but weak because the \citet{Mouginot2019} calibration and \citet{Mankoff2021} time-series share the same underlying raw data and with independent processing methodology. The IMBIE-3 time-series is a combination of 42 independent mass balance measurements from satellite observations of glacier flow, surface elevation, and gravitational anomaly. Our model falls within error of the validation data for discharge and total mass balance (Fig. \ref{fig:greenland_delay_fit}). The validation results suggest that our simple model, with two fitted parameters and a selected lag, captures the total contribution of GrIS discharge to GMSLR during the observational record and its sensitivity to ocean temperatures in Greenland's peripheral waters.

The fitted $\delta = 5$~yr is an effective lag for spatially averaged forcing and we therefore do not attempt to attribute it to a single process. It lies within the range of timescales spanned by the ocean-to-ice pathway, from exchange between the continental shelf and fjord interiors through the terminus response to undercutting and the inland transmission of retreat to the flux gates at which discharge is measured \citep{StraneoHeimbach2013,Wood2021,robel_response_2018}. Because $T_{\mathrm{ocean}}$ is averaged over Greenland's peripheral waters, $\delta$ also absorbs the time required for a warming anomaly to become representative of conditions at outlet glaciers distributed around the ice sheet. 

To our knowledge, no published study reports a Greenland-wide sensitivity of discharge rate to ocean temperature, so we derive a simple dimensional scaling to contextualize the inferred value $\gamma = 0.365$~mm\,SLE\,yr$^{-1}$\,$^\circ$C$^{-1}$. For a single outlet glacier $i$, the submarine melt rate scales approximately linearly with ocean thermal forcing, the ocean temperature above the in situ freezing point $T_{\mathrm{ocean}}$, such that
\begin{equation}
	\Delta \dot{m}_i = \kappa_i T_{\mathrm{ocean}},
	\label{eq:melt_rate}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa_i$ is the melt sensitivity to thermal forcing averaged over the submerged calving face. Plume parameterizations calibrated against Greenland observations \citep{Xu2013, Rignot2016, Slater2022} give a maximum melt sensitivity near the grounding line of 90--260~m\,yr$^{-1}$\,$^\circ$C$^{-1}$. The face-averaged rate is two to three times smaller \citep{Wood2021}. We therefore take $\kappa = 36$--$104$~m\,yr$^{-1}$\,$^\circ$C$^{-1}$, with median 60~m\,yr$^{-1}$\,$^\circ$C$^{-1}$. Submarine melt thins and undercuts the terminus, triggering calving and grounding-line retreat so that the total ice flux perturbation is a factor $\mu$ times the direct submarine melt flux. For Greenland's tidewater glaciers, observations and models suggest $\mu = 3$--$10$ \citep{Enderlin2013, Wood2021}. The flux perturbation from a single glacier with submerged calving-front area $A_f$ is then
\begin{equation}
	\Delta \dot{Q}_i = \mu_i\,\rho_{ice}\,A_{f,i}\,\kappa_i T_{\mathrm{ocean}},
	\label{eq:single_glacier_flux}
\end{equation}
where $\rho_{ice} = 900$~kg\,m$^{-3}$ is the mass density of ice. Summing over Greenland's $N = 219$ marine-terminating outlets \citep{Mouginot2019} we approximate the scale of $\gamma$ as
\begin{equation}
	\widetilde{\gamma} \sim \frac{1}{A_{\mathrm{ocean}}\,\rho_{fw} T_{\mathrm{ocean}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \Delta\dot{Q}_i ,
	\label{eq:gamma_aggregate}
\end{equation}
where $A_{\mathrm{ocean}} = 3.625 \times 10^{14}$~m$^2$ is the ocean area and $\rho_{fw} = 1000$~kg\,m$^{-3}$ is the density of freshwater, which converts added ice mass to sea-level equivalent under the convention 1~mm\,SLE~$= 362.5$~Gt used throughout this study. Submerged calving-front areas of 0.9--2.7~km$^2$ are reported for five West Greenland outlets \citep{Rignot2016}. Evaluating Eq.~\ref{eq:gamma_aggregate} across the full parameter ranges gives
\begin{equation}
	\widetilde{\gamma} = 0.05\text{--}1.5~\mathrm{mm\,SLE\,yr}^{-1}\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}^{-1},
	\label{eq:gamma_estimate}
\end{equation}
which contains our calibrated $\gamma = 0.365$~mm\,SLE\,yr$^{-1}$\,$^\circ$C$^{-1}$. We report Eq.~\ref{eq:gamma_estimate} as a rough order-of-magnitude check on the inferred value intended for intuition building. The range spans a factor of 30 because it is the product of four quantities that are not measured in aggregate, and the factorization $\sum_i \mu_i A_{f,i} \kappa_i \approx N\,\bar{\mu}\,\bar{A}_f\,\bar{\kappa}$ assumes these are uncorrelated across outlets, whereas the deepest and widest fronts occupy the warmest fjords. Finally, the calibrated $\gamma$ is an effective sensitivity that absorbs the covarying atmospheric pathway because increased subglacial runoff amplifies plume-driven melting alongside ocean warming. 



\subsubsection{Thermosteric}

The ocean is the dominant reservoir for the excess heat accumulated by the climate system \citep{Cheng2017}, and the resulting expansion -- the thermosteric contribution -- is currently the largest single contributor to observed sea level rise \citep{Frederikse2020}. Both heat exchange between ocean and atmosphere and ocean dynamics set the structure of a simple model of the thermosteric effect. Put simply, the ocean warms with a delay relative to the atmosphere, heat enters at the sea surface and is carried downward by turbulent mixing and by the overturning circulation, causing temperature of the interior to reflect the history of surface warming. Downward transfer spans a wide range of timescales. The upper few hundred meters exchange heat with the atmosphere over years to decades, whereas water below the thermocline is renewed over centuries. We therefore represent the ocean as two layers, a fast upper layer coupled to the atmosphere and a slow deep layer coupled only to the upper layer, following the structure of \citet{Geoffroy2013}. This structure reproduces both the lag between surface warming and expansion and the continued expansion that follows a stabilization of surface temperature, where the deep layer draws heat from the upper layer after the upper layer has equilibrated.

Let us first consider an upper layer of thickness $h_u=700$ m warmed by $\Delta\theta(z,t)$, which raises steric height of the ocean over time as
	\begin{equation}
	H_{\mathrm{u}} = \int_0^{h_u} \alpha_{sw} \Delta\theta\,dz,
	\label{eq:steric_integral}
	\end{equation}
where $\alpha_{sw}(z)$ is the thermal expansion coefficient of seawater, which is depth dependent due to its temperature dependence. The temperature anomaly profile is defined as $\Delta\theta = S_u\varphi$, where $S_u(t)$ is the amplitude of the anomaly and $\varphi(z)$ is a dimensionless shape factor. Expanding $\alpha_{sw}(z) = \alpha_{0_{sw}}(z) + \alpha'_{sw}\Delta\theta$, with $\alpha'_{sw} = \partial\alpha_{sw}/\partial\theta$, we rewrite Eq. \ref{eq:steric_integral} as 
	\begin{equation}
	H_{\mathrm{u}} = a_u S_u^2 + b_u S_u + \epsilon_u(t - t_0),
	\label{eq:thermo_upper}
	\end{equation}
where $a_u = \int_0^{h_u} \alpha'_{sw}\varphi^2dz$ and $b_u = \int_0^{h_u} \alpha_{0_{sw}}\varphi dz$. The linear term $b_u$ is the expansion of the upper layer for a given warming while the quadratic term $a_u$ comes from the temperature dependence of $\alpha_{sw}$. We tacked on $\epsilon_u(t - t_0)$ to absorb instrument drift during calibration. As GMST, $T(t)$, changes, the amplitude evolves as
	\begin{equation}
	\frac{dS_u}{dt} = \frac{T - S_u}{\tau_u},
	\label{eq:thermo_su}
	\end{equation}
where $\tau_u$ is the characteristic time over which the thermal state $S_u$ relaxes to GMST. Equation~\ref{eq:thermo_su} shows that $S_u$ is an average of past temperatures, weighted toward the recent past over a time window set by $\tau_u$. The upper layer therefore smooths year-to-year fluctuations in GMST and follows longer trends with a delay comparable to $\tau_u$. 

The deep layer follows the same construction, with $\Delta\theta_d(z,t) = S_d(t)\,\varphi_d(z)$ over 700--2000 m depth and $b_d = \int_{h_u}^{h_d} \alpha_{0_{sw}}\,\varphi_d\,dz$, with $h_d=2000$ m. We drop the quadratic term because $S_d$ remains small enough that the temperature dependence of $\alpha_{sw}$ can be neglected. Warming from GMST reaches the deep layer only after passing through the upper layer, so the thermal state $S_d$ relaxes toward $S_u$ as
      \begin{equation}
      \frac{dS_d}{dt} = \frac{S_u - S_d}{\tau_d},
      \label{eq:thermo_sd}
      \end{equation}
where we fixed $\tau_d = 150$ yr at the CMIP5 multi-model mean deep-ocean adjustment time
\citep[][Table 4]{Geoffroy2013}, because the 21-year 0--2000\,m data record is too short to constrain a centennial relaxation time. Adding this layer gives the total steric response for the upper 2000 m as,
      \begin{equation}
      H_{ud}(t) = H_{\mathrm{u}}(t) + b_d\,S_d,
      \label{eq:thermo_total}
      \end{equation}
which we fit jointly to the 0--700\,m and 0--2000 m records. Finally, to account for expansion below 2000 m depth, we define 
	\begin{equation}
	H_{\mathrm{full}}(t) = H_{ud}(t) + \dot{H}_{b}(t - t_{\mathrm{ref}}),
	\label{eq:thermo_full}
	\end{equation}
where $\dot{H}_{b} = 0.07 \pm 0.04$\,mm\,yr$^{-1}$ \citep{Purkey2010} is a constant rate for depths below 2000\,m, referenced to year $t_{\mathrm{ref}} = 2000$. This layer is not observed at annual resolution, so its rate is adopted rather than calibrated.

We fit the two-layer model in Eq.~\ref{eq:thermo_total} jointly to NOAA in situ thermosteric sea level estimates at 0--700\,m (1955--2025) and 0--2000\,m (2005--2025) \citep{Levitus2012}. We use Bayesian inference \citep{Tarantola2005} in which MCMC samples parameter combinations $\boldsymbol{\phi} = (a, b_u, b_d, \epsilon_u, \tau_u, \sigma_{\mathrm{extra}})$ with probability density proportional to the posterior (Fig.~\ref{fig:ocean_priors_posteriors}). Assuming the residual at each point is normally distributed about the model with variance $\sigma_i^2$, the negative log-likelihood takes the form $\frac{1}{2}\sum_i \left[(r_i(\boldsymbol{\phi})/\sigma_i)^2 + \ln\sigma_i^2\right]$. Adding a negative log-prior for $\tau_u$, whose log-normal prior (defined below) has mean $\mu_{\tau_u}$ and standard deviation $s_{\tau_u}$ in $\ln\tau_u$, gives the objective function
      \begin{equation}
      \mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\phi}) = \sum_{i} \left[\left(\frac{r_i(\boldsymbol{\phi})}{\sigma_i}\right)^2 + \ln\sigma_i^2\right] + \left(\frac{\ln\tau_u - \mu_{\tau_u}}{s_{\tau_u}}\right)^2,
      \label{eq:thermo_objective}
      \end{equation}
which is twice the negative log-posterior up to an additive constant. MCMC visits parameter combinations $\boldsymbol{\phi}$ with probability proportional to $\exp[-\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\phi})/2]$. Here, $r_i(\boldsymbol{\phi})$ is the residual between the demeaned model and demeaned observation at point $i$, and $\sigma_i^2$ is that point's total variance. The point variance is defined by the quadrature sum of the formal measurement error, a structural uncertainty retained after a bias correction in the NOAA thermosteric observations \citep{Li2022}, and $\sigma_{\mathrm{extra}}$, a noise term, shared across both the 0--700 m and 0--2000 m records, that absorbs misfit beyond the reported observational error. The MCMC sampling uses package emcee with 64 walkers, 6000 burn-in steps, and 30,000 production steps to ensure convergence. The relaxation time of the upper later, $\tau_u$, has an informative prior, $\log(\tau_u) \sim \mathcal{N}(\ln 8,0.5^2)$, or $\mu_{\tau_u} = \ln (8$ yr) and $s_{\tau_u} = 0.5$ yr, consistent with the range in \citet{Geoffroy2013}. The model parameters $b_u$ and $\tau_u$ are correlated in the likelihood---a slower upper-layer response can be partly offset by a larger sensitivity to fit the same trajectory---but this partial degeneracy is managed by the prior on $\tau_u$. Five parameters --- $a$, $b_u$, $b_d$, $\epsilon_u$, and $\log(\sigma_{\mathrm{extra}})$ --- get uninformative uniform priors with wide bounds (Fig. \ref{fig:ocean_priors_posteriors}). The posterior median and 90\% credible intervals are: $a = 20.0$ [10.1, 31.2] mm $^\circ$C$^{-2}$, $b_u = 51.0$ [40.6, 75.4] mm $^\circ$C$^{-1}$, $b_d = 68.6$ [37.0, 104.3] mm $^\circ$C$^{-1}$, $\epsilon_u = 0.023$ [$-0.112$, 0.140] mm yr$^{-1}$, $\tau_u = 11.6$ [6.2, 25.3] yr, and $\sigma_{\mathrm{extra}} = 2.0$ [1.7, 2.3] mm. The calibrated model reproduces both records with RMS residuals of 2.3 mm for depths 0--700 m and 1.3 mm for 0--2000 m, against 42 and 30 mm of observed rise over the respective records.

\subsubsection{Glaciers}

The rate of glacier mass loss has been shown to be approximately linear with warming \citep{Rounce2023, GlaMBIE2025}. In the interest of developing consistent uncertainty quantification for this study, we perform our own Bayesian inference of the cumulative glacier sea-level contribution using the Glacier Mass Balance Intercomparison Exercise (GlaMBIE) time-series from 2000--2023. In level space, the model is
\begin{equation}
	H_{\mathrm{gl}}(t) = b_{gl}\int_{t_0}^{t}Td\tau + c_{gl}(t - t_0) + H_{gl_0} + \varepsilon_{gl}(t),
	\label{eq:glacier_level}
\end{equation}
where $T$ is the GMST anomaly, evaluated at the annual resolution of the GlaMBIE calendar-year-mean, $t_0$ is the study's baseline year (2000) when $H_{gl_0}$ is the model's level, and $\varepsilon_{gl}(t)$ is jointly distributed as $\mathcal{N}(0,\, \Sigma_{gl})$, with $\Sigma_{gl}$ the true covariance matrix of the cumulative data, built directly from the GlaMBIE annual uncertainties. We apply weakly informative priors $b_{gl}\sim \mathcal{N}(0.61,\,2.0)$ mm yr$^{-1}$ $^\circ$C$^{-1}$, truncated at $b_{gl}\geq0$, and $c_{gl} \sim \mathcal{N}(0.3,1)$ mm yr$^{-1}$. The prior mean for $b_{gl}$ is drawn from \citet{Rounce2023}, whose reported end-of-century glacier contributions of 0.70 to 2.23 mm yr$^{-1}$ across warming scenarios of 1.5 to 4 $^\circ$C imply a sensitivity near 0.61 mm yr$^{-1}$ $^\circ$C$^{-1}$. The standard deviation is wide enough that the prior is only weakly informative, and the non-negativity truncation recognizes that glaciers lose mass as the climate warms. For the MCMC, we use package emcee with 32 walkers, 2000 burn-in steps, and 4000 production steps. The posterior median sensitivity is $b = 0.75$ mm yr$^{-1}$ $^\circ$C$^{-1}$ [0.30--1.20] and the committed rate is $c = 0.57$ mm yr$^{-1}$ [0.45--0.69]. %, with a reduced chi-square of 1.04 against the reported GlaMBIE uncertainties confirming that this correlated error model is well specified (Fig. \ref{fig:component_fit_diagnostics}a, Fig. \ref{fig:glacier_corner}).

\subsubsection{Antarctic Peninsula}
\label{s:peninsula}

The Antarctic Peninsula component uses the same level-space Bayesian framework as glaciers, fit at annual resolution to the IMBIE-3 Peninsula time-series spanning 1979--2023 (45 annual points; \citealp{Otosaka2026}), with the rate--temperature model $\dot{H}_{\mathrm{p}} = a_pT^2 + b_pT + c_p$. The Peninsula contribution in this work reflects combined SMB and glacier discharge responses to warming. As with glaciers, $H_{p_0}$ is anchored at the record's own first point (1979), and the true covariance of this cumulative record, built from the IMBIE-3 uncertainties, is used in the likelihood. Unlike glaciers, we also fit a model-inadequacy term $\sigma_{p,\mathrm{extra}}$ to account for annual scatter beyond the IMBIE-3 reported uncertainty. The emcee implementation uses 32 walkers, 2000 burn-in steps, and 4000 production steps. We fit both the linear ($a_p=0$) and quadratic model with priors $b_p\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\,2.0)$ mm yr$^{-1}$ $^\circ$C$^{-1}$ and $c_p \sim \mathcal{N}(0.3,1)$ mm yr$^{-1}$. The Bayesian Information Criterion favors the linear model with posterior median sensitivity $b_p = 0.044$ mm yr$^{-1}$ $^\circ$C$^{-1}$ [$-0.020$, 0.108] and committed rate $c_p = 0.056$ mm\,yr$^{-1}$ [0.039, 0.072] (Fig. \ref{fig:component_fit_diagnostics}b). The confidence interval spans zero, so these data do not, on their own, resolve the sign of the temperature sensitivity. 


\subsubsection{East Antarctica}

The EAIS component follows the same corrected level-space framework as glaciers and the Peninsula, fit at annual resolution to the IMBIE-3 EAIS record (1979--2023, 45 annual points; \citep{Otosaka2026}), with $H_{e_0}$ anchored at the record's own first point and the true covariance of the cumulative record, built from the IMBIE-3 uncertainties. The linear sensitivity $b_e$ is given a symmetric prior, $b_e \sim \mathcal{N}(-0.17,\,0.5)$ mm\,yr$^{-1}$\,$^\circ$C$^{-1}$. The prior mean is drawn from an independent literature surface-mass-balance sensitivity ($C_T = 60\pm20$ Gt\,yr$^{-1}$\,$^\circ$C$^{-1}$; \citealp{Frieler2015,Ligtenberg2013}). The posterior sensitivity is $b_e = -0.015$~mm\,yr$^{-1}$\,$^\circ$C$^{-1}$ [$-0.14$, 0.12] and committed rate $c_e = 0.007$~mm\,yr$^{-1}$ [$-0.03$, 0.04], with a reduced chi-square of 0.91 against the reported IMBIE-3 uncertainties (Fig. \ref{fig:component_fit_diagnostics}c). 

\subsubsection{West Antarctica}

Unlike glaciers, the Antarctic Peninsula, and EAIS, we do not fit a rate--temperature regression to WAIS over the observational record. We instead fit a quadratic in time to the 1979--2023 IMBIE-3 mass balance time series \citep{Otosaka2026}. This fit uses the same Bayesian framework as \S\ref{s:peninsula} but replacing temperature with time. The acceleration $a$ is given a symmetric prior, $a \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\, \sigma=0.05\ \mathrm{mm\ yr^{-2}})$. The quadratic yields an acceleration of 0.012 mm yr$^{-2}$ [0.011--0.013] and a rate in year 2000 of 0.230 mm yr$^{-1}$ [0.215--0.246] (Fig.~\ref{fig:component_fit_diagnostics}d). We set the rate of WAIS loss to zero prior to 1979, when the IMBIE-3 time series begins and the fit to the data shows a rate $<0.05$ mm/yr.


\subsection{Forecasting methods and validation}
\label{s:forecasting_model}

The forecasts presented here use the IPCC AR6 GMST trajectories for different emission scenarios, though they can be applied to any GMST trajectory. We focus on the policy-relevant emission scenarios, which are labeled by their approximate median end-of-century global mean surface temperature anomaly (relative to preindustrial) rounded to the nearest integer: 2$\,^\circ$C (SSP1-2.6), 3$\,^\circ$C (SSP2-4.5), and 4$\,^\circ$C (SSP3-7.0). We initialize the GMSL forecast using the GMSL level, rate, and acceleration observed in the satellite-era and blend in the component-model sum in rate space, such that
\begin{equation}
	\dot{H}_{\mathrm{forecast}} = w\dot{H}_{\mathrm{obs}} + \bigl[1 - w\bigr]\dot{H}_{\mathrm{comp}},
	\label{eq:blend_rate}
\end{equation}
where $\dot{H}_{\mathrm{obs}}$ is the rate from the satellite-era quadratic fit of \citet{Hamlington2024,Nerem2022}, $\dot{H}_{\mathrm{comp}}$ is the rate of the sum of the calibrated components. The blending weight $w(t)$ is a sigmoid
\begin{equation}
	w = 1 - \Theta\left(\frac{t - t_r}{\tau_r}\right),
	\label{eq:sigmoid_weight}
\end{equation}
where $\Theta(x) =(1 + e^{-x})^{-1}$ is the standard logistic function, $t_r = 2035$, and $\tau_r = 5$ yr. At $t \ll t_r$, $w \approx 1$ and the forecast follows the observed quadratic trajectory. At $t \gg t_r$, $w \approx 0$ and the forecast follows the component models. The transition spans approximately 22 years, meaning that 10\%--90\% of the weight shift occurs over $\pm 2.2 \tau_r \approx \pm 11$ yr about $t_r$. We chose $t_r = 2035$ because it is more than a quarter and less than a half of the GMSL satellite altimetry record into the future, a period when the extrapolation of observations should have strong forecasting skill. The tapering recognizes the expected reduction in forecasting skill of the simple data extrapolation over timescales longer than roughly half of the observational window. Pushing $t_r$ to 5 years later (approximately half of the calibration window and arguably the latest viable choice) produces forecasts that are indistinguishable within uncertainty, suggesting the results are largely insensitive to the specific value of $t_r$ within the range of 10-15 years into the future.  

The GMSL forecast is obtained by integrating Eq.~\eqref{eq:blend_rate} forward from the observed level at the forecast origin ($t_0 = 2025$, the most recent NASA altimetry data as of this writing), so that 
\begin{equation}
	H_{\mathrm{forecast}}(t) = H_{\mathrm{obs}}(t_0) + \int_{t_0}^{t} \dot{H}_{\mathrm{forecast}}(t')\,dt'.
	\label{eq:blend_level}
\end{equation}
The blending is done sample-by-sample across the 2000-member Monte Carlo ensemble, preserving correlations between the quadratic-fit parameter uncertainty and the component-model uncertainty. Because the extrapolation is derived entirely from the satellite altimetry record, it carries no dependence on emissions pathway. The component sum, $\dot{H}_{\mathrm{comp}}$, is SSP-dependent through the temperature-forced component models. Consequently, the blended forecast is SSP-independent in the near term (where $w \approx 1$) and becomes progressively SSP-dependent as the component physics dominate at later times.  

Uncertainty in $\dot{H}_{\mathrm{comp}}$ is propagated by Monte Carlo summation of the components. Each component has its own $2000$-member ensemble of level-space projections $H_k(t)$, drawn independently from that component's posterior or, for components using prescribed literature sensitivities rather than data calibration, from the corresponding parameter distributions. The total level is formed sample-by-sample, $H_{\mathrm{comp},i}(t)=\sum_k H_{k,i}(t)$ for $i=1,\dots,2000$, so draws from independently calibrated components are summed without pairing individual samples by any shared physical driver. The component rate $\dot{H}_{\mathrm{comp}}$ used in Eq.~\eqref{eq:blend_rate} is obtained by finite-differencing this summed ensemble in time before blending with the observational quadratic rate. Reported credible intervals (66\% and 90\%) are the 17th/83rd and 5th/95th empirical percentiles of the resulting ensemble at each time step.

\subsubsection{Component-level GMST dependence}

Some of the SPECTRUM model components described in Section \ref{s:component_model_derivation} lack direct, explicit dependences on GMST. These components include the Greenland glacier discharge, which depends on the temperature of the ocean surrounding Greenland, and the SMB of Greenland and EAIS. 

To project GrIS discharge model forward in time, we calibrate a transfer function between the Greenland regional surface temperature anomaly and the ocean temperature and then apply a normal distribution of Arctic amplification factors to calculate the Greenland regional surface temperature from projected GMST under different emission scenarios. The linear transfer function on annual-mean observations is
\begin{equation}
	T_{\mathrm{ocean}}(t) = \xi T_{\mathrm{surface}}^{\mathrm{Gr}}(t) + \chi + \varepsilon_{go}(t),
	\label{eq:ocean_transfer}
\end{equation}
where $T_{\mathrm{surface}}^{\mathrm{Gr}}$ is the Greenland regional surface temperature anomaly and $\varepsilon_{go}$ is a zero-mean residual. Both series are anomalies relative to the 1995--2005 baseline. An OLS fit over $52$ common annual values gives $\xi = 0.148 \pm 0.027$, $\chi = -0.051 \pm 0.031\,^\circ$C, and residual standard deviation $\sigma_{\varepsilon_{go}} = 0.20 ^\circ$C. 

For projections, SSP GMST trajectories are converted to Greenland regional surface temperature using an Arctic amplification factor $AA= 3.0 \pm 0.5$, consistent with the observed ratio of Greenland-to-global warming over 1970--2021. Greenland regional surface temperatures \citep{Rohde2020} are used in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ocean_transfer} to obtain projected $T_{\mathrm{ocean}}$, which then enters Eq.~\eqref{eq:greenland_dyn} via the delayed integral. Each of the 2,000 Monte Carlo samples draws $\mathrm{AA} \sim \mathcal{N}(3.0, 0.5^2)$, $\xi \sim \mathcal{N}(0.148, 0.027^2)$, and $\chi \sim \mathcal{N}(-0.051, 0.031^2)$ jointly with $(\gamma, r_0)$ from the discharge posterior, with the ocean-temperature delay held fixed at $\delta = 5$~yr, so that Arctic amplification, transfer-function, and discharge parameter uncertainties all propagate into the projections. A single draw of $(\xi, \chi, \gamma, r_0)$ is applied to all SSP scenarios, so that inter-SSP differences isolate the effect of the emissions pathway from parameter uncertainty.

For SMB in Greenland and EAIS, the forecast uses simple thermodynamic models calibrated from regional climate models. For Greenland SMB, the forecast uses a quadratic temperature scaling $\dot{H}_{\mathrm{smb}} = C_{T^2}\Delta T^2 + C_T \Delta T + \dot{M}_0$ with sensitivities drawn from the spread between regional climate models \citep{Fettweis2020, Noel2021, Glaude2024}. The linear sensitivity $C_T \sim \mathcal{N}(-300, 80^2)$~Gt yr$^{-1}$ $^\circ$C$^{-1}$ of GMST encompasses the MAR, RACMO, and HIRHAM estimates. The quadratic sensitivity, $C_{T^2} \sim \mathcal{N}(-50, 30^2)$~Gt yr$^{-1}$ $^\circ$C$^{-2}$, represents the effects of ablation zone expansion and melt-albedo feedback with warming. $\dot{M}_0 = 380$~Gt yr$^{-1}$ is the baseline SMB. For EAIS SMB, we use a linear model ($C_{T^2}=0$) and the literature-derived accumulation sensitivity $C_T = 60 \pm 20$~Gt yr$^{-1}$ $^\circ$C$^{-1}$ of GMST \citep{Frieler2015, Ligtenberg2013}, reflecting the Clausius--Clapeyron scaling of snowfall with warming ($C_T > 0$ corresponds to EAIS mass gain through accumulation). Data from IMBIE-3 show EAIS in approximate balance (Fig. \ref{fig:component_fit_diagnostics}c), so we set $\dot{M}_0 =0$. The EAIS contribution is small ($-13$ to $-30$~mm at 2100, depending on scenario) relative to the total, meaning the GMSL projection results of this study are largely insensitive to these parameter choices.

Terrestrial water storage projections are adopted directly from the IPCC AR6 rather than independently modeled, as the processes governing groundwater depletion, reservoir impoundment, and land hydrology changes are outside the scope of this framework and the data are insufficient to allow for a data-driven approach as with other components. At each decadal interval in the IPCC projections, we fit a Gaussian distribution to the reported median and inter-quantile spread and draw 2,000 Monte Carlo samples, which are then linearly interpolated to annual resolution. For the hindcast and budget-closure diagnostics we use the \citet{Frederikse2020} TWS estimate with Monte Carlo sampling from their reported uncertainties, but only from 2003 onward. Their TWS is derived from GRACE and GRACE-FO observations over 2003--2018, partitioned from the total land-mass signal; before 2003 it is a composite of a reconstruction of climate-driven water storage and process models for reservoir impoundment and groundwater depletion rather than a measurement. We therefore set TWS to zero before 2003, rebased so the cumulative contribution is continuous across the transition, and we note that the pre-2003 component sum consequently omits a real contribution to sea level. TWS varies little among scenarios and its contribution is small relative to the total, so it does not materially affect the uncertainty budget.



\subsubsection{West Antarctic Ice Sheet} 

West Antarctica dominates the uncertainty in sea-level projections because its major outlet glaciers, particularly Thwaites and Pine Island, lie close to a threshold at which small changes in ice geometry could trigger self-sustaining retreat \citep{Joughin2014,PattynMorlighem2020,Fricker2025}. Whether that threshold has been, or will be, crossed depends on glacier-specific details that remain only partly observed. The underlying mechanism, known as the Marine Ice Sheet Instability (MISI), was first proposed several decades ago \citep{Mercer1968,Weertman1974} and later formalized in idealized settings \citep{Schoof2007}. In essence, MISI could arise where glaciers rest on beds that are below sea level and deepen inland, which allows thinning to float the ice off the seafloor, reducing basal drag, and allowing further acceleration and thinning. The assumptions that drive runaway in idealized models do not necessarily map to the present day West Antarctic Ice Sheet, where various processes complicate straightforward application of the MISI concept. For example, ice shelves, the floating extensions of the ice sheet, buttress the glaciers upstream of them \citep{Gudmundsson2013,Pegler2018,HaseloffSergienko2018}. The magnitude of buttressing, and how it evolves as the glacier retreats, depends on the geometry of the bed and shelf, factors that vary across West Antarctica \citep{SergienkoWingham2019,SergienkoHindmarsh2013}. Buttressing can evolve on multi-annual timescales as recently evinced by direct observations of the Pine Island Ice Shelf that show a near-total loss of buttressing stress over roughly the past decade, accompanying a 20\% increase in glacier velocity \citep{WellsMoran2026}, and ongoing evolution of the Thwaites ice shelf \citep{Alley2021}. Retreat of glaciers also can be driven independently by sustained ocean warming melting the ice from below \citep{Jenkins2018,Davis2023}, a process nominally distinct from a genuinely self-sustaining runaway (MISI) in that it depends on continued oceanic heat delivery rather than on geometry and buoyancy alone. In practice, however, the distinction between an internally driven runaway retreat and one driven by continued oceanic heating is not clear cut because the two can produce near-identical retreat over the coming decades while implying markedly different outcomes over subsequent centuries \citep{Sergienko2026,Hill2023,Reese2023}. Recent regional ocean modeling finds that such heat delivery is itself largely committed over the coming decades, regardless of near-term emissions pathways \citep{Naughten2023}, and current melt rates may already be sufficient to have triggered self-sustaining retreat independently of any further increase in forcing \citep{ArthernWilliams2017}. This combination of a physically plausible positive feedback mechanism, glacier-specific buttressing whose variability is difficult to predict, and the challenge of distinguishing reversible from irreversible retreat using present-day data, together make West Antarctica's future contribution to GMSL the largest and most consequential source of uncertainty in current projections \citep{Bamber2019}. As a result, current projections of West Antarctica's GMSL contribution span a wide range, from several centimeters to several tens of centimeters by 2100 under high-emissions scenarios, according to the multi-model assessments underlying IPCC AR6 \citep{Fox-Kemper2021,Seroussi2020,Edwards2021}. 

%This range arises less from disagreement over future warming and more from uncertainties in the physics of the ice sheet. Ice-sheet models differ substantially in their treatment of buttressing, basal melting, and grounding-line dynamics, such that forcing different models with the same climate scenario yields markedly different outcomes even before observational uncertainty is considered \citep{Payne2021,Seroussi2020}. This structural disagreement between models, rather than uncertainty in the forcing, is now understood to be the dominant source of spread \citep{Edwards2021}, and yet is incomplete, having not been fully explored due to the absence of systematic sensitivity studies in ice sheet models. One example of an unexplored source of uncertainty arising from how models represent the dynamics of glaciers is the stress exponent $n$ governing the nonlinear viscosity of ice in the flow law. Nearly all ice-sheet models assume $n = 3$, although laboratory experiments and observations suggests that $n = 4$ may better represent ice flow in relevant areas \citep{Millstein2022,Ranganathan2024}. This single parameter choice markedly accelerates projected loss from the Amundsen Sea Embayment and produces sea-level-rise rates that are highly sensitive to its value across a range of model benchmarks \citep{GetraerMorlighem2025,Martin2026}. Furthermore, a small number of models incorporate more speculative mechanisms, such as the rapid structural failure of tall ice cliffs following the loss of their supporting ice shelf. This process, broadly known as the Marine Ice Cliff Instability (MICI), can raise projected contributions substantially, toward a meter or more by 2100 \citep{DeContoP2016,DeConto2021}. However, the physical basis and likelihood of this mechanism remain actively debated \citep{Clerc2019,Morlighem2024,SunGudmundsson2026}, and most standard projections either exclude MICI or treat it as a low-probability tail. Model divergence is compounded by model initialization itself, which currently relies on initializing to snapshots or model spin-ups \citep{Seroussi2020,Fricker2025}. 

Given these complexities and uncertainties, we do not attempt to constrain a GMST sensitivity for WAIS. Such an effort is beyond the scope of this work. Instead, we develop a two-scenario mixture model to represent a range of potential GMSL contributions from WAIS in the 21st century. Based on growing evidence that WAIS mass loss is primarily sensitive to internal ice dynamics and deep ocean temperatures that are likely to change slowly in the coming decades, we make our WAIS projections independent of the warming trajectory. Thus, the range of 21st-century outcomes is controlled by ice dynamics that are insensitive to emissions over our forecasting horizon in the SPECTRUM model, consistent with previous studies \citep{Naughten2023,Coulon2024,Rosier2025}. 

In Scenario 1 (S1), mass loss continues along the observed trajectory (Fig. \ref{fig:component_fit_diagnostics}d). Extrapolating the trend gives a median GMSL contribution of 84 mm by 2100, closely matching the median of the ISMIP6 emulator, 85 mm \citep{Edwards2021}. The ISMIP6 emulator is the only model in AR6 with a symmetric distribution. This median falls between the modes of the LARMIP-2 assessment \citep{Levermann2020} --- with mode 73 mm, median 137 mm, and skewness 2.42 --- and the structured expert judgment \citep{Bamber2019}, with mode 120 mm, median 207 mm, and skewness 2.40. A 45-year observational record cannot itself constrain how much additional error should accumulate from extrapolating a further 76 years beyond it, so a confidence interval built from the fitted parameters alone would understate the true uncertainty in this extrapolation. We instead treat the reported growth in uncertainty beyond the last observation in the ISMIP6 emulator as an external estimate of the extrapolation error, applying it as a prediction error following \citet{Tarantola2005}, and add it to our parameter uncertainty. This widens our 90\% CI at 2100 to [$-13$, 180] mm, closely matching the range of the ISMIP6 emulator of [$-11$, 182] mm. The S1 probability distribution, exceedance curve, and time-series are shown in Fig \ref{fig:wais_p_s1_sweep} by the curve with the probability of S1, p(S1), at 100\%.

Scenario 2 (S2) is intended to represent a future where mass loss can accelerate well beyond the current observed rates. We define the distribution in year 2100 with a lower bound (5th percentile) of 84 mm, equal to the S1 median. The S2 upper bound (95th percentile) is 1.3 m, approximately the IPCC AR6 low-confidence Antarctic Ice Sheet 95th percentile under SSP5-8.5 in the year 2100 \citep{Fox-Kemper2021}. The distribution is right-skewed, and defined as skew-normal in log-space with shape parameter $\lambda=3$, reflecting the expectation that instability skews outcomes toward greater loss \citep{Robel2019}. A sensitivity analysis indicates that the results we present are largely insensitive to the chosen value of $\lambda$ within the expected reliability of the distributions. The S2 probability distribution, exceedance curve, and time-series are shown in Fig \ref{fig:wais_p_s1_sweep} as the p(S1) = 0 curve. 

The final probability density is a simple mixture of S1 and S2. Its median, 95th percentile, and other quantiles are determined primarily by p(S1) and the upper bound (95th percentile) of S2. These two mixing parameters are closely related in a simple mapping to the median and 95th percentile of the mixture (Fig. \ref{fig:wais_median_heatmap}), which allows a single control variable for most use cases (Fig \ref{fig:wais_p_s1_sweep}). The trajectory of WAIS mass loss through time is set by a power law in time that starts at the observed rate and accelerates toward the 2100 value, with median exponent $\beta = 2$, chosen so the S2 95th percentile stays within the IPCC AR6 medium-confidence range for the Antarctic Ice Sheet until about 2050 and exceeds it afterward. We chose p(S1) as the control variable and present two cases representing reasonable end members: a `slow WAIS' mass loss case where p(S1) = 90\% and `fast WAIS' with p(S1) = 10\%.

\subsection{Validation} 
\subsubsection{Total GMSL budget closure}
\label{s:budget_close}

Satellite altimetry data collected from 1993--2025 provide observations of GMSL \citep{Beckley2017,Hamlington2020} that can be compared with the sum of the independently calibrated model components (Fig. \ref{fig:budget_closure}). Total GMSL is withheld from calibration to serve as a validation data set. For Greenland SMB, which we do not calibrate due to data sparseness, we use RACMO-derived SMB \citep{Mouginot2019}. Terrestrial water storage is adopted from \citet{Frederikse2020} from 2003 onward, the period over which their estimate derives from GRACE observations, and set to zero before.

The component sum closes 96--103\% of the observed GMSLR rate over three epochs (1993--2025, 2000--2025, 2010--2025), offering validation that the individually calibrated components together approximate GMSL during the instrumental record. Because each component is calibrated independently, we treat their rate uncertainties as independent and combine them in quadrature. Each component's individual uncertainty comes directly from its Monte Carlo ensemble. For a given epoch, we fit a linear trend to every ensemble member and take the standard deviation of the resulting trends. Repeating this for each epoch lets the combined uncertainty differ from one epoch to the next, rather than using one fixed value throughout the satellite record. By this measure, the residual is within one combined standard deviation of the component rate uncertainties in all three epochs (0.79$\sigma$, 0.83$\sigma$, and 0.88$\sigma$ for 1993--2025, 2000--2025, and 2010--2025, respectively).

\subsubsection{Hindcast of the level, rate, and transient sensitivity}

We validate the combined GMST-sensitive model by two hindcasting approaches. Both are generated by forcing the model components with observed GMST back to 1900 \citep{Rohde2020}, with an emphasis on post-1950 when increasingly reliable GMSL data are available. The first comparison is between global mean sea level and rate produced by SPECTRUM with data from \citet{Fox-Kemper2021} and \citet{Frederikse2020}. For the second comparison, we calculate the transient sensitivity of the SPECTRUM output and GMSL data, where the transient sensitivity is defined as $\alpha = d\dot{S}/dT$ with $\dot{S}$ as the rate of change in GMSL. Here, we use the same rate-space blending scheme as the forecast (\S\ref{s:forecasting_model}), reflected about the forecast origin so that the satellite-era quadratic dominates at late times and the component models at early times, with $t_r = 2015$ and $\tau_r = 5$ yr. This gives the observational term the same weight at 2025 that it carries at the forecast origin, and reduces it to 0.05 by 2000, so the hindcast over the reconstruction era tests the component models rather than restating the altimetry extrapolation. %While a hindcast analysis should be seen as a necessary condition for presenting a forecast, the sensitivity test is arguably the more important validation because a sea-level projection model is simply a way of representing the sensitivity of GMSL to warming to allow for calculations of GMSL given a GMST forcing.

Extending the hindcast back to 1900 shows two distinct regimes relative to the GMSL reconstructed time-series (Fig. \ref{fig:observations}a). Over the satellite era the hindcast tracks the observations closely, with a 1993--2025 rate of 3.19 mm yr$^{-1}$ against 3.35, 3.17, and 3.23 mm yr$^{-1}$ for the \citet{Frederikse2020} and \citet{Dangendorf2024} reconstructions and the IPCC AR6 observational compilation \citep{Fox-Kemper2021}, and with the satellite altimetry record \citep{Beckley2017}. Earlier in the record it understates the observed rise. Over 1950--2000 the hindcast rate is 0.74 mm yr$^{-1}$ against 1.02, 1.01, and 1.26 mm yr$^{-1}$ for the same three records, and the hindcast level at 1950 sits 13--25 mm above them, indicating too little accumulated rise over the second half of the twentieth century. Before 1950, where only the \citet{Frederikse2020} and \citet{Dangendorf2024} reconstructions extend, the divergence grows: the hindcast rate is 0.27 mm yr$^{-1}$ against 1.39 and 1.64 mm yr$^{-1}$ over 1900--1950, and the hindcast level at 1900 is 79--85 mm above the reconstructions, relative to the 2000 baseline. The exclusion of terrestrial water storage before 2003 does not account for this shortfall; because impoundment made that term a negative contribution through the dam era, omitting it moves the hindcast toward the reconstructions rather than away. This divergence coincides with the period in which the reconstructions rely increasingly on sparse tide-gauge coverage and model-based infilling and our model suffers from a lack of constraints on mass changes in the Greenland Ice Sheet and glaciers. The limitations of our model arise by design: we chose to focus only on model representations that can be fit to observations and that represent the ongoing response to warming. This choice excludes more complete and complex land-ice process representations that would be necessary to represent GMSL during colder periods in the past, as this is not our goal. We therefore treat the pre-1950 hindcast as qualitatively informative but do not draw quantitative conclusions from it. We restrict our validation claims to the 75-year, post-1950 period, where component calibration data are available.

The transient sensitivity of our component model, which defines the change in the rate of change in GMSL with warming, tracks closely with the observed sensitivities (Fig. \ref{fig:observations}b). It is worth emphasizing that this agreement includes the transition from the earlier period when thermosteric sea-level rise was the largest contributor to GMSL to the present when the sum of land-ice and terrestrial water storage (barystatic) are larger overall contributors to GMSL. The transient sensitivity of the observations and model also agree with the semi-empirical results of \citet{Rahmstorf2007} (Fig. \ref{fig:observations}b), which continue to track observations nearly 20 years after publication, showing that the simple semi-empirical model continues to provide a reasonable first-estimate of GMSL rise with warming. 

Together, the level, rate, and transient-sensitivity comparisons indicate that our framework reproduces the observed behavior of GMSLR over a 75-year window (1950--2025) spanning 1.1--1.2 $^\circ$C of GMST change, without having been calibrated on observations of GMSL itself. This window includes two forms of agreement and one shortfall. The hindcast reproduces the satellite-era rate (3.19 mm yr$^{-1}$ over 1993--2025, against 3.17--3.35 mm yr$^{-1}$ in the observational records), and its transient sensitivity reproduces both the observed rate--temperature curvature and its underlying physical cause (the shift from thermosteric to barystatic dominance). It understates the 1950--2000 rate, however, giving 0.74 mm yr$^{-1}$ against 1.01--1.26 mm yr$^{-1}$, which we attribute to the absence of observational constraints on glaciers and the Greenland Ice Sheet before the satellite era. To our knowledge, no other GMSL forecasting framework or model presents this level of detailed validation or hindcast skill. Because this validation window is comparable in duration and magnitude of warming to the near- and mid-term forecast horizon, it supports extending the same confidence forward over a comparable span, through 2100 and a further 1--2 $^\circ$C of warming, consistent with the taper used to blend observed and component-model trends in the forecast itself (\S\ref{s:forecasting_model}). This validated envelope does not, however, extend to processes for which comparable historical constraints do not exist, which is why WAIS, the largest source of forecast uncertainty, is treated separately through a scenario-based `storyline', rather than data-driven approach, in this work.

The thermosteric contribution is 143 [114, 183], 242 [203, 291], and 370 [303, 452] mm at 2, 3, and 4 $^\circ$C, respectively (Fig. \ref{fig:per_component_forecast}b). The corresponding AR6 medians for thermal expansion are 142, 197, and 249 mm. On the shared 1995--2014 baseline our 2 $^\circ$C projection matches AR6, a ratio of 0.96. Our median then rises to 20\% above the AR6 median at 3 $^\circ$C and 46\% above at 4 $^\circ$C. Two features of the two-layer model \citep{Geoffroy2013} drive that divergence. The quadratic term in the upper-layer expansion carries the temperature dependence of the thermal expansion coefficient of seawater, so each added degree of warming expands the upper ocean more than the last. The deep layer also draws heat from the upper layer throughout the century, consistent with observed deep-ocean warming \citep{Purkey2010,Cheng2017}. 

The Greenland Ice Sheet contributes 122 [92, 152], 169 [122, 214], and 218 [152, 283] mm at 2, 3, and 4 $^\circ$C (Fig. \ref{fig:per_component_forecast}c). The AR6 medians are 55, 83, and 114 mm. Ours are 1.9 to 2.2 times as large, and our 5th percentile exceeds the AR6 median in every scenario. An independently calibrated Greenland posterior \citep{Aschwanden2022} also sits above AR6, and its stated envelope of 4 to 30 cm contains our 90\% intervals in all three scenarios. The RCP scenarios of that study correspond only approximately to our SSP scenarios, and RCP8.5 is closer to SSP5-8.5 than to the SSP3-7.0 pathway of our 4 $^\circ$C case. Our Greenland discharge model is calibrated against the observed discharge record \citep{Mouginot2019}, with independent mass-balance records withheld for validation \citep{Mankoff2021,Otosaka2026}. Greenland projections calibrated on observations therefore run high relative to the ISMIP6-based process-model ensembles that underpin AR6 \citep{Seroussi2020,Edwards2021}. The ocean-forced discharge pathway is the likely source of the difference \citep{Slater2020,Wood2021}.

We project glacier contributions of 114 [88, 141], 138 [100, 180], and 162 [108, 216] mm at 2, 3, and 4 $^\circ$C (Fig. \ref{fig:per_component_forecast}d). The AR6 medians are 89, 123, and 157 mm. On the shared 1995--2014 baseline our ratios to AR6 are 1.25, 1.10, and 1.01, so our glacier projection converges on the AR6 median as warming increases. \citet{Rounce2023} report the glacier contribution over 2015--2100 relative to 2015, binned by 2100 warming. Our 2015 level is 9.7 mm above the 2000 baseline. Over that window we project medians of 104, 128, and 152 mm, against their ensemble medians of $99 \pm 31$, $125 \pm 39$, and $154 \pm 44$ mm, reported as median and 95\% confidence interval. The agreement holds in all three scenarios. The rate of glacier mass loss is approximately linear with warming in the GlaMBIE record that we calibrate to \citep{GlaMBIE2025}, consistent with glacier model ensembles \citep{Rounce2023,Marzeion2020}. Our glacier warming-sensitivity prior is centered on 0.61 mm yr$^{-1}$ $^\circ$C$^{-1}$, drawn from \citet{Rounce2023}, so this comparison is a consistency check. The prior is weakly informative and the GlaMBIE record sets the posterior.

The combined Antarctic Peninsula and EAIS contribution stays near zero, with medians of $-4.5$ [$-13.6$, 4.4], $-8.7$ [$-21.7$, 3.9], and $-12.7$ [$-29.0$, 3.2] mm at 2, 3, and 4 $^\circ$C (Fig. \ref{fig:per_component_forecast}e). Negative values denote a sea-level fall. All three 90\% intervals span zero, so the sign of the net contribution is not resolved. The EAIS contribution falls from $-13.5$ to $-24.5$ mm across the three scenarios as the accumulation response strengthens, partly offset by a Peninsula contribution rising from $+8.9$ to $+11.6$ mm. The EAIS projection adopts a literature surface-mass-balance sensitivity, $C_T = 60 \pm 20$ Gt yr$^{-1}$ $^\circ$C$^{-1}$ \citep{Frieler2015,Ligtenberg2013}. The IMBIE-3 regression serves as a diagnostic check on that value \citep{Otosaka2026}. The Peninsula sensitivity is calibrated to the IMBIE-3 Peninsula record \citep{Otosaka2026}. It reflects both surface melt and the dynamic response to ice-shelf collapse \citep{Scambos2004}. West Antarctica is treated separately below.

\subsection{Orthogonal dimensions of sea-level risk}

The first risk dimension is sensitive to emissions reduction. The temperature-dependent components are sensitive to changes in global mean surface temperature. Here, we calibrate the sensitivities using decades of observations. Their collective contribution shifts systematically across warming trajectories and is reducible by emissions cuts (Fig. \ref{fig:components_voi}a). Under the `slow WAIS' scenario, the 90\% range of the forecast narrows to 21 cm at 2$\,^\circ$C, 23 cm at 3$\,^\circ$C, and 29 cm at 4$\,^\circ$C, four to five times narrower than the full forecast that admits rapid mass loss from WAIS driven by internal feedbacks (Fig.~\ref{fig:ridge_projections}). Emissions policy becomes the primary lever for managing risk in that world. Moving from 2$\,^\circ$C to 4$\,^\circ$C adds 36 cm of rise, exposing 61 million more people to coastal flood risks and adding US\$49B/yr in coastal adaptation costs.

The second risk dimension is relatively insensitive to emissions reductions. WAIS dynamics accounts for virtually all of the total forecast variance and is largely decoupled from the emission pathway. The tail of the forecast distribution---the outcomes that drive the most severe human and economic consequences---is controlled almost entirely by whether WAIS retreat remains gradual at modern rates of loss or transitions to a more rapid, irreversible retreat. In this case, emissions reduction decreases the median but does not substantively change the tail of the distribution. The probability of exceeding 2 m relative to pre-industrial is 3\% under the 2$\,^\circ$C trajectory and 6\% under 4$\,^\circ$C, with a scenario-averaged probability of 4.5\%. The 3\% lower bound on this range highlights the fact that emission reductions alone cannot retire the high risk outcomes.

The risks and costs to coastal communities helps to highlight the dichotomy between the two possible scenarios of fast vs slow WAIS mass loss, or equivalently, emissions-insensitive vs emissions sensitive (Figs. \ref{fig:ridge_projections} and \ref{fig:components_voi}a). The difference at the 5\% exceedance probability, one possible design threshold for coastal adaptation that we denote by stars in Fig. \ref{fig:ridge_projections}b, is 0.95 m of sea-level rise by 2100 under the current policy warming trajectory (3 $^\circ$C). This difference translates into roughly 177M more people exposed to annual flood risks, US\$127B/yr in coastal adaptation, and US\$12.7T/yr ($\sim$12\% global GDP per year) in coastal flood damages without further adaptation. These large numbers reflect global population densities, with the highest densities near coastlines, and the fact that the locations of coastal communities and infrastructure are calibrated to lower sea levels. For intuition, the fast-WAIS scenario at 5\% exceedance implies nearly eight times more sea level rise in the next 75 years than was experienced in the past 125 years, while the slow-WAIS scenario implies a factor of 3.5 more rise.

Resolving the question of WAIS mass loss rates is among the most financially consequential goals in modern science (Fig.~\ref{fig:components_voi}c). A value-of-information analysis shows that perfect knowledge of whether WAIS will lose mass slowly or rapidly is worth of order US\$5T in avoided costs, with the value peaking when confidence in a slow-WAIS future reaches 75\% and the information arrives early enough to inform infrastructure decisions. The value decays with time because late-arriving information leaves fewer years of decisions to improve, and because all forecasts, including this study, degrade as they extend beyond their calibration bounds. These numbers frame the research investment question in concrete terms: even partial reduction of WAIS uncertainty, well short of perfect knowledge, would justify annual research expenditures orders of magnitude above current levels. The return on that investment is determined by how quickly the observations can narrow the range of WAIS outcomes, which depends on both the rate of data collection and the development of reliable process models, like those used in the IPCC, capable of providing explanatory insights that underscore deep understanding of ice sheets and climate. 

Our forecast identifies a single measurement that would do more to reduce sea-level uncertainty than any other: the rate of West Antarctic ice loss over the next two decades. If WAIS mass loss continues near current rates through the 2030s and 2040s, that would build the case for the slow-WAIS scenario and allow the forecast to narrow toward the predictable, lower, emissions-sensitive range where adaptation costs are more manageable. If instead grounding-line retreat accelerates beyond the pace of our central scenario, the distribution would shift toward higher outcomes and 1.5--2 m of rise would move from a tail risk to a planning baseline. Either outcome would reshape coastal adaptation worldwide and would justify investment well beyond current levels. The observations needed to distinguish these futures can be made within a generation, but only if the observing systems and research programs are sustained and expanded at a scale that reflects the costs at stake.

Scientific research is a necessary condition for providing actionable information for coastal adaptation, but it is as likely as not that further research will add to the growing evidence to support rapid WAIS mass loss rather than build confidence in a slow-WAIS future \citep{Fricker2025}. Sustaining a wide range of uncertainties that admit $<0.8$ m and $> 1.9$ m of GMSLR in 2100 at 5\% probabilities, will force decisions on coastal planning, adaptation, and managed retreat that impact millions of lives and cost billions of dollars to be made under large uncertainties. The extent of these uncertainties in terms of lives, property, and ecosystems raises the question: Can we take action to increase the potential stability of West Antarctica? How much harm to people and the environment can we prevent? Recently, some research directions have been proposed to answer this question \citep{MacAyeal2024} with initial models of glacier interventions aimed at stabilizing WAIS show promise \citep{Meyer2026, Miller2026}. As with any nascent research, there are many unresolved questions about the feasibility, unintended consequences, and governance of these interventions \citep{Siegert2025}. There are risks in intervening to remediate the West Antarctic Ice Sheet and slow its loss and the logistical challenges will undoubtedly be great, but these risks and challenges must be weighed against the risks and challenges of sea-level rise as described in this study. There is no zero risk, zero cost response to sea level rise. The costs and challenges are real and must be borne regardless of what decisions are made. The questions are how much cost, how much challenge, and how much harm. The answers hinge on how fast West Antarctica will lose ice, and nothing short of a virtual guarantee in slow WAIS mass loss provides comfort for the world's coastlines.   


\section{Conclusions}

SPECTRUM forecasts 21st-century sea-level rise from parsimonious physical models of the individual contributors to sea level. We calibrate the contributors that have long observational records by Bayesian inference against those records and adopt the remaining sensitivities from published work. West Antarctic discharge is represented instead as a weighted mixture of scenarios, because the observational record cannot yet distinguish a self-sustaining retreat from one driven by ocean melting. Observed global mean sea level is withheld from every component calibration and reserved as a test of the assembled framework. Forced with observed temperatures, the summed components reproduce global mean sea level in hindcast tests, with the model median falling within the uncertainty of the observational reconstructions after 1940, a period spanning about 1 $^\circ$C of warming. The hindcast rate over 1993--2025 is 3.19 mm yr$^{-1}$, against 3.17--3.35 mm yr$^{-1}$ in the observational records. The summed components also reproduce the observed sensitivity of the rate of rise to warming. Because that window is comparable in duration and in warming magnitude to the forecast horizon, the hindcast supports carrying the same confidence forward through 2100 and a further 1--2 $^\circ$C of warming. That support covers the calibrated components. It does not extend to West Antarctic discharge, which lacks a comparable historical constraint and is carried instead as the scenario mixture.

Driven forward with warming trajectories, the framework projects medians and 90\% credible intervals at 2100, relative to the year 2000, of 0.62 [0.44, 1.55] m at 2 $^\circ$C, 0.78 [0.60, 1.71] m at 3 $^\circ$C, and 0.99 [0.77, 1.91] m at 4 $^\circ$C. Referenced to the AR6 baseline, those medians run 38 to 44\% above the IPCC AR6 medium-confidence projections \citep{Fox-Kemper2021}.

The uncertainty in these forecasts has two sources. The first is the set of contributors whose temperature sensitivity we calibrated against observations. These account for roughly 1\% of the forecast variance and 10 to 20\% of the width of the 90\% credible interval. Their contribution shifts systematically with warming, and emissions cuts reduce it. The second is the West Antarctic Ice Sheet. It accounts for nearly all of the forecast variance and for 80 to 90\% of the width of the 90\% credible interval. It responds weakly to the emissions pathway. Holding warming to 2 $^\circ$C rather than 4 $^\circ$C lowers the median by 0.37 m but moves the probability of exceeding 2 m only from 6\% to 3\%. Emissions policy sets how much rise is likely. West Antarctica sets how bad the worst case gets.

The two sources differ in what they cost and in what can be done about them. Every centimeter of rise from either source exposes roughly 1.6 million more people to annual coastal flooding, and about 90\% of them live in low- and middle-income countries \citep{Kulp2019, Jongman2012}. Resolving whether West Antarctica loses mass slowly or rapidly is worth on the order of US\$5T in avoided adaptation costs. That value is largest when confidence in a slow-WAIS future reaches 75\% and when the answer arrives early enough to shape infrastructure decisions. The answer will come from the rate of West Antarctic ice loss over the next two decades. Emissions cuts limit how far sea level rises. Sustained observation of West Antarctica tells coastal communities how much rise to prepare for. Neither retires the tail. Whether anything can, through intervention aimed at slowing West Antarctic ice loss, is an open research question \citep{MacAyeal2024, Meyer2026, Miller2026} carrying unresolved problems of feasibility, unintended consequences, and governance \citep{Siegert2025}. Those risks are to be weighed against the risks quantified here. There is no zero-risk, zero-cost response to sea-level rise.
