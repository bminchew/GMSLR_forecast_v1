\section{Materials and Methods}

Unless otherwise noted, all reported intervals are 90\% Bayesian credible intervals (equal-tailed), and where $1\sigma$ is reported, we state explicitly that it is a standard deviation.




\subsection{Observational datasets}

All component models are calibrated against published observational products (Table~\ref{tab:obs_datasets}). We use:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Global mean sea level:} NASA satellite altimetry (TOPEX/Jason, 1993--2025; \citealp{Beckley2017,Hamlington2020}), with the Frederikse et~al.~\citeyearpar{Frederikse2020} tide-gauge reconstruction (1900--2018) and the Dangendorf et~al.~\citeyearpar{Dangendorf2024} reconstruction (1900--2021) for pre-satellite validation. None of these records enters any component calibration.
\item \textbf{Ocean thermal expansion:} NOAA thermosteric sea level (doi:10.7289/V53F4MVP), 0--700\,m (1955--2025) and 0--2000\,m (2005--2025) \citep{Levitus2012}. Both records are scaled by 1.14 before fitting. This corrects the 14\% low bias that \citet{Li2022} find in thermosteric trends computed with linear vertical interpolation. A residual structural uncertainty of 5\% of the corrected signal is retained. The 700--2000\,m layer is carried by the 0--2000\,m record from 2005 onward and by the deep layer of the two-layer model at earlier times. A constant $0.07 \pm 0.04$~mm\,yr$^{-1}$ \citep{Purkey2010, Desbruyeres2016} is added over the full record for the contribution below 2000\,m. Two reconstructions of global-mean steric sea level are withheld for validation, from Frederikse et~al.~\citeyearpar{Frederikse2020} (1900--2018) and Dangendorf et~al.~\citeyearpar{Dangendorf2024} (1900--2021). Over 1957--2018 the Frederikse et~al.~reconstruction draws on the same archive of in situ profiles as the NOAA record \citep{Levitus2012, Ishii2017, Cheng2016}, so it is not fully independent of it there. The Dangendorf et~al.~reconstruction uses no in situ ocean temperature or salinity. Its steric term is the residual of a sea-level budget whose barystatic input is from \citet{Frederikse2020}. Neither reconstruction carries the 1.14 scaling.
\item \textbf{Glaciers:} GlaMBIE global consensus (The GlaMBIE Team~\citeyear{GlaMBIE2025}), combining glaciological field measurements, satellite altimetry, DEM differencing, and GRACE/GRACE-FO gravimetry across all 19 RGI regions. We use 2000--2023 (24 annual points) for calibration.
\item \textbf{Greenland:} \citet{Mouginot2019} SMB and discharge (1972--2018), of which the 44 years from 1975 enter the discharge fit, the earlier years being excluded because the lagged ocean forcing is undefined before then. \citet{Mankoff2021} discharge (1986--2023) and the IMBIE-3 Greenland record (1972--2023; \citet{Otosaka2026}) are withheld for validation. Subsurface ocean temperature from EN4 v4.2.2 with g10 bias corrections (200--500\,m, Greenland peripheral waters, 1970--2021; \citet{Good2013}). Surface temperature from Berkeley Earth \citep{Rohde2020}. Greenland SMB is projected with a statistical emulator of the regional climate model MARv3.12 driven by seven CMIP6 global climate models (CESM2, MPI-ESM1-2-HR, NorESM2-MM, UKESM1-0-LL, CNRM-CM6-1, CNRM-ESM2-1, and IPSL-CM6A-LR). These simulations were produced for the PROTECT project \citep{Boberg2026} and are publicly available at \url{http://ftp.climato.be/fettweis/MARv3.12/Greenland/PROTECT}. We use the annual SMB of the contiguous ice sheet, excluding peripheral glaciers, over 1980--2014 and over each available SSP to 2100, together with the global mean surface temperature of each driving model from the CMIP6 archive. \citet{Mankoff2021} SMB (1986--2023), the mean of three regional climate models driven by reanalysis, is withheld for validation of the emulator.
\item \textbf{Antarctica:} IMBIE-3 mass balance (1979--2023, 45 annual points per region; \citet{Otosaka2026}), partitioned into West Antarctica, East Antarctica, and the Antarctic Peninsula.
\item \textbf{Terrestrial water storage:} \citet{Frederikse2020} reconstruction, used only from 2003 onward, the period over which it derives from GRACE and GRACE-FO observations rather than from models. Prior to 2003, the reconstructed TWS curves are largely unconstrained by independent observations. 
\item \textbf{Temperature scenarios:} IPCC AR6 assessed GMST projections (SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, SSP5-8.5; \citet{Lee2021}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Component model posteriors}
\label{s:component_posteriors}

This section collects the parameter posteriors of every calibrated component model (Figs.~\ref{fig:greenland_discharge_posteriors}--\ref{fig:eais_corner}). Greenland discharge is fitted by weighted least squares over a discrete set of delays, so its posterior is a mixture of normals weighted by the Bayesian Information Criterion and is shown as marginal densities. The remaining components are sampled by Markov chain Monte Carlo and are shown as corner plots, with marginal histograms on the diagonal and pairwise joint densities on the off-diagonal. Posterior medians and their 90\% credible intervals are reported in the main text, and they are the values carried into the projections.

\subsection{Greenland surface mass balance emulator}
\label{s:greenland_smb_emulator}

We project Greenland SMB with a statistical emulator of the regional climate model MARv3.12 driven by seven CMIP6 global climate models \citep{Boberg2026}: CESM2, MPI-ESM1-2-HR, NorESM2-MM, UKESM1-0-LL, CNRM-CM6-1, CNRM-ESM2-1, and IPSL-CM6A-LR. For each GCM, we fit the annual SMB contribution of the ice sheet as a quadratic in that GCM's own GMST anomaly $T$, relative to 1995--2005 and smoothed with an 11-year centered mean,
\begin{equation}
\dot{H}_{\mathrm{smb}} = a_{gris}\,T^2 + b_{gris}\,T + c_{gris} ,
\label{eq:smb_emulator}
\end{equation}
with $\dot{H}_{\mathrm{smb}}$ in mm\,SLE\,yr$^{-1}$ and positive for sea-level rise. Each fit pools the history run (1980--2014) with every SSP run available for that GCM (2015--2100). Each run is a separate segment with first-order autoregressive residuals. The posterior of $a_{gris}$, $b_{gris}$, and the intercept is analytic under a weak normal-inverse-gamma prior, and the autocorrelation is integrated over a grid weighted by marginal likelihood. We tested adding a lag between warming and the SMB response for CESM2 and MPI-ESM1-2-HR, the two GCMs with all three scenarios. It did not improve the fits, so the emulator has none.

In projections, we replace the intercept fitted to MAR with $c_{gris}$, the observed 1995--2005 SMB contribution of \citet{Mouginot2019}. The emulator supplies the change in SMB with warming, and the observations supply its present level. Year-to-year weather makes an 11-year average of observed SMB uncertain. After removing the long-term trend, Mouginot SMB over 1972--2018 varies from year to year with a standard deviation of 96~Gt\,yr$^{-1}$, and consecutive years are weakly correlated (0.27). An 11-year mean of such a record has a standard deviation of 37~Gt\,yr$^{-1}$, or 0.10~mm\,SLE\,yr$^{-1}$. We therefore draw $c_{gris} = -1.03 \pm 0.10$~mm\,SLE\,yr$^{-1}$ (1$\sigma$) separately for each member, where $-1.03$~mm\,SLE\,yr$^{-1}$ is the observed 1995--2005 mean of 374~Gt\,yr$^{-1}$. Each member also draws one GCM, with equal weight, and one posterior draw of $a_{gris}$ and $b_{gris}$ for that GCM. Because MAR is the only regional climate model in the ensemble, the spread between regional climate models is not sampled. Under the same forcing, RACMO gives less SMB loss than MAR \citep{Glaude2024}.

%A single quadratic in $T$ describes every scenario of a given GCM (Fig.~\ref{fig:greenland_smb_fit}). Within each GCM, $a_{gris}$ and $b_{gris}$ are strongly anticorrelated, with correlation coefficients of $-0.93$ to $-0.95$, so the coefficients are best compared through the SMB contribution they imply. At $T = 3\,^\circ$C, the medians range from 1.23~mm\,SLE\,yr$^{-1}$ (MPI-ESM1-2-HR) to 1.68~mm\,SLE\,yr$^{-1}$ (UKESM1-0-LL) above the 1995--2005 level.

Driven by observed GMST, the emulator's forced response declines more slowly than the observed SMB (Fig.~\ref{fig:greenland_smb_hindcast}). Its median SMB falls from 387~Gt\,yr$^{-1}$ over 1972--1985 to 358~Gt\,yr$^{-1}$ over 2010--2018, while the \citet{Mouginot2019} SMB falls from 466 to 252~Gt\,yr$^{-1}$. Every Mouginot year lies within the emulator's 90\% range once weather noise is included, and 3 of the 38 years of the withheld \citet{Mankoff2021} SMB lie outside it (2010, 2012, and 2019). The emulator represents the response of SMB to global warming. It does not reproduce multi-year departures from that response, such as the low SMB of 2000--2018.

\subsection{Variance decomposition}

We decompose the variance of the 2100 forecast separately for each warming trajectory. Pooling the trajectories would require assigning each one a probability. The total here is the sum of the component models before blending with the observed trend, excluding terrestrial water storage. Under the fast-WAIS weighting, WAIS accounts for 98.7\%, 96.9\%, and 93.1\% of the total variance at 2, 3, and 4\,$^\circ$C. The thermosteric, glacier, and Greenland contributions together account for 1.1\%, 2.9\%, and 6.9\%, a share that includes the covariance among them from the warming path they share. EAIS and the Antarctic Peninsula account for the small remainder. WAIS does not depend on the warming trajectory or on the parameters of any other component, so these shares nearly sum to one.

\subsection{Temperature scenarios}

The component projections are driven by the IPCC AR6 projections of global mean surface temperature for each SSP \citep{Lee2021}. Each SSP has a median trajectory and a 5--95\% range, which comes mostly from uncertainty in the response of the climate to a given emissions pathway. A world that warms faster than the median tends to stay warmer for the whole century. We therefore treat this range as a set of warming paths rather than as independent noise in each year.

Each ensemble member $k$ is assigned one value $z_k$ drawn from a standard normal distribution. Its temperature is the median trajectory plus an offset $\delta_k(t) = z_k\,[h(t) - h(t_0)]$ for $t > t_0$, and zero before $t_0$. When $z_k > 0$, $h(t)$ is the distance from the median to the 95th percentile divided by 1.645. When $z_k < 0$, it is the distance from the 5th percentile to the median divided by 1.645. This keeps the asymmetry of the AR6 range. We set $t_0 = 2024$ because warming up to the present is observed, so the offset adds spread only to future warming. As a result, the spread at 2100 is somewhat narrower than the full AR6 range. For SSP2-4.5 the upper half-width at 2099 is 0.64$\,^\circ$C rather than 0.82$\,^\circ$C.

Member $k$ uses the same $z_k$ in every component and every SSP. Each member therefore represents one world, in which the ocean, the glaciers, and the ice sheets all respond to the same warming. The $z_k$ are drawn with their own random seed, so the parameter draws of each component do not change. Sampling the warming range widens the 2100 intervals of the temperature-driven components, most strongly for thermal expansion. At 3$\,^\circ$C its 90\% interval grows from 87 to 156\,mm.

\subsection{Component-level skill metrics}

Table~\ref{tab:skill_metrics} collects the goodness-of-fit statistics for every component fitted to data. We report reduced chi-square rather than a coefficient of determination. Every component is calibrated against a cumulative record whose variance is dominated by its trend, so $R^2$ in that space approaches unity for any model that reproduces the trend at all and says little about the quality of the fit. A straight line in time, with no temperature dependence whatsoever, scores 0.983 against the 0--2000\,m thermosteric record.

Reduced chi-square compares the residuals with the uncertainty the calibration data are reported to carry, so a value near unity indicates both that the model reproduces the observations to within their stated errors and that the error model used in the likelihood is well specified. The normalization is not identical in every case, and we note where it differs. Glaciers, the Antarctic Peninsula, and EAIS are fitted to cumulative records whose errors are serially correlated, and their $\chi^2$ is therefore evaluated against the full covariance of the cumulative record built from the reported annual uncertainties, not against a diagonal approximation, which would understate the residual scatter that the reported errors can absorb. All three sit between 0.91 and 1.06. The Peninsula reaches that value only with a fitted model-inadequacy term $\sigma_{p,\mathrm{extra}}$, described in the Antarctic Peninsula section of the main text. Without $\sigma_{p,\mathrm{extra}}$, the reduced chi-square is 2.64, so the IMBIE-3 Peninsula data carries real annual scatter beyond its reported per-year uncertainty. Glaciers and EAIS do not need model-inadequacy terms. For the thermosteric model the quoted value includes the fitted excess-noise term $\sigma_{\mathrm{extra}}$ in the point variance and is normalized by the number of data points rather than by the degrees of freedom, so it sits slightly below the corresponding $\chi^2/\mathrm{dof}$. The 0--2000 m value of 0.36 indicates that the 21-year record is fitted to well inside its reported error. The Greenland discharge value of 1.83 is the generalized chi-square of the demeaned cumulative fit before the error scale is recalibrated. It is the quantity by which the parameter covariance is subsequently inflated, so it is a statement about the reported errors being too small rather than about the model missing the data. It has 41 degrees of freedom (= 44 fitted points minus one for the demeaning of the cumulative record and two for the fitted parameters). The \citet{Mouginot2019} discharge record runs from 1972, but the delayed ocean forcing is undefined before 1975 because EN4 begins in 1970 and the selected lag is 5\,yr, so the first three years of the discharge record do not enter the fit.

Two components need further comment. WAIS is fitted in time rather than in temperature, so no statistic comparable with the other rows is available for it. A weighted least-squares cross-check on the same record, using diagonal rather than correlated weights, gives $\chi^2 = 38.8$ on 44 annual points with three free parameters, and favors a cubic in time over the quadratic by $\Delta\mathrm{BIC} = -4.1$. We retain the quadratic because the purpose of the WAIS fit is to set the starting rate and acceleration for the Scenario 1 extrapolation rather than to identify the polynomial order best supported by a 45-year record, and a cubic extrapolated to 2100 is governed by a term the observations constrain only weakly. Snowfall anomalies also modulate the record. In the Amundsen Sea Embayment, persistently low snowfall added $51 \pm 4$~Gt\,yr$^{-1}$ to the mass loss during 2009--2013, and extreme snowfall reduced it by $60 \pm 16$~Gt\,yr$^{-1}$ in 2019 and 2020 \citep{Davison2023}. To test their effect on the fit, we removed both anomalies from the IMBIE-3 record, converting at 362.5~Gt per mm SLE, and refitted the quadratic by weighted least squares. The same weighted least-squares fit to the unaltered record reproduces the Bayesian estimates, with an acceleration of 0.0140~mm\,yr$^{-2}$ and a contribution of 93~mm at 2100 relative to 2000. With the anomalies removed, the acceleration falls to 0.0123~mm\,yr$^{-2}$, the rate in 2000 falls from 0.226 to 0.212~mm\,yr$^{-1}$, and the contribution at 2100 falls to 83~mm. This test is approximate, because \citet{Davison2023} report the anomalies for the Amundsen Sea Embayment alone and as multi-year means. The 10~mm change is small relative to the S1 90\% interval of [$-3$, 191]~mm, so we fit the record as reported.EAIS is listed as a diagnostic because the East Antarctic Ice Sheet is close to balance over the IMBIE-3 record, leaving little signal for a temperature-dependent model to recover. The fit is consistent with the reported errors, but the projection adopts a literature surface-mass-balance sensitivity, $C_T = 60 \pm 20$~Gt\,yr$^{-1}$\,$^\circ$C$^{-1}$ \citep{Frieler2015,Ligtenberg2013}, and uses the regression only as a check on that value.

One further fit enters the framework without being a component model in its own right. The linear surface-to-ocean transfer function given in the main text, which carries SSP temperature trajectories into the Greenland discharge model, explains a modest fraction of the interannual variance, $R^2 = 0.38$ over 52 common annual values, with a residual standard deviation of 0.20\,$^\circ$C that is propagated into the projections. It is the weakest statistical link in the Greenland projection chain, and it is retained rather than replaced because the quantity it must supply is the multi-decadal trend in subsurface temperature rather than its year-to-year variability. Greenland SMB, by contrast, is not fitted to observations. It is passed through as the RACMO-derived \citet{Mouginot2019} SMB over the historical period and projected with the emulator of MARv3.12 described in Section~\ref{s:greenland_smb_emulator}. Its out-of-sample test is the leave-one-GCM-out comparison in Fig.~\ref{fig:greenland_smb_lomo}.

These are all within-sample statistics, and on their own they establish only that each model is consistent with the record it was fitted to. The tests that bear on forecasting skill are out of sample. The thermosteric model is compared against the \citet{Frederikse2020} steric reconstruction, withheld from calibration, with an RMS difference of 2.6\,mm over 1957--2018 and 3.8\,mm before 1957, where that reconstruction is itself constrained by models rather than by subsurface observations. Greenland discharge is compared against the \citet{Mankoff2021} record (1986--2023) and the IMBIE-3 time series \citep{Otosaka2026}, neither of which enters the fit. The model falls within error of both for discharge and for total mass balance. As noted in the main text, the \citet{Mankoff2021} comparison is the weaker of the two, because that record shares its underlying raw data with the \citet{Mouginot2019} calibration and differs from it in processing rather than in provenance. IMBIE-3 is the more informative test. It combines mass-balance estimates from satellite observations of ice flow, surface elevation, and gravimetry. Its input-output estimates, which are built from ice flow, share data with the \citet{Mouginot2019} calibration, but its altimetry and gravimetry estimates are independent of it. When the discharge model is refitted to the 1990--2018 part of the \citet{Mouginot2019} record alone, it gives $\gamma = 0.365$ [0.302, 0.426]~mm\,SLE\,yr$^{-1}$\,$^\circ$C$^{-1}$ and predicts 19.00 [18.64, 19.36]~mm SLE of cumulative discharge over the withheld years 1975--1990, against 19.04 [18.69, 19.38]~mm SLE observed. The WAIS quadratic reaches 8.8 $\pm$ 0.6\,mm at 2024 against the IMBIE-3 value of 9.0 $\pm$ 0.6\,mm. The tests for the framework as a whole rather than one component at a time are budget closure, in which the summed components are compared with satellite altimetry measurements that no component calibration saw, and the hindcast of the level, rate, and transient sensitivity of GMSL since 1950. Both are presented in the Results of the main text.

\section{Value of Information Analysis}
\label{sec:voi}
%% ================================================================

The value of information (VoI) analysis quantifies the economic benefit of resolving the binary uncertainty about whether WAIS retreat remains slow (Scenario 1) or allows for the possibility of rapid retreat (Scenario 2). We compute the expected value of perfect information (EVPI), the perfect-information limit of the sample-information framework of \citet{Raiffa1961}. No signal model is specified, so any test that discriminates the two scenarios imperfectly would return less than the values reported here.

\subsection{Decision framework}

We consider an illustrative coastal adaptation decision where a planner must choose a defense height $d$ (meters of sea-level protection) to minimize total expected costs, evaluated against the sea-level rise distribution $S$ at a representative target year of 2100. For a given sea-level rise distribution $S$ at 2100, the total annual cost of defense height $d$ is
\begin{equation}
C(d) = C_{\mathrm{adapt}}(d) + \mathbb{E}\bigl[D\bigl(\max(S - d,\, 0)\bigr)\bigr],
\label{eq:total_cost}
\end{equation}
where $C_{\mathrm{adapt}}(d) = c_a \cdot d$ is the annualized adaptation cost and $\mathbb{E}\bigl[D(\cdot)\bigr]$ is the expected value of the residual damage $D$ from SLR exceeding the defense height (overtopping).

\subsubsection{Adaptation cost}
\label{s:adaptation_cost}

The total capital cost of coastal adaptation is estimated at $C_{\mathrm{cap}} = \$4$\,T/m of GMSLR. This is derived from Tiggeloven et~al.~\citeyearpar{Tiggeloven2020}, who estimate that 3.4\% of the global coastline ($\sim$54{,}400~km of $\sim$1.6~million~km total) warrants dike heightening under cost-benefit optimality, at a unit cost of \$7\,M per km per meter of dike height (the median cost raising dikes in New Orleans post-Hurricane Katrina). This yields $\sim$\$400\,B/m for dike raising alone along the optimal 3.4\% of coastline. We apply a conservative 10$\times$ multiplier to account for the full societal adaptation burden---including seawalls, nature-based solutions, managed retreat, and infrastructure relocation---across a broader fraction of the global coastline.

Because the cost function $C(d)$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:total_cost}) combines adaptation costs with annual flood damages from Jevrejeva et~al.~\citeyearpar{Jevrejeva2018}, both terms need to be expressed in consistent units. Adaptation is dominated by capital investment deployed over the planning horizon, so we conservatively annualize $C_{\mathrm{cap}}$ using the capital recovery factor (CRF):
\begin{equation}
c_a = C_{\mathrm{cap}} \times \mathrm{CRF}, \qquad \mathrm{CRF} = \frac{r\,(1+r)^n}{(1+r)^n - 1},
\label{eq:crf}
\end{equation}
where $r = 0.03$ is the real discount rate and $n = 75$\,yr is the planning horizon. This gives $\mathrm{CRF} \approx 0.034$ and an annualized adaptation cost of $c_a \approx \$135$\,B/(m$\cdot$yr) of GMSLR. The resulting $C(d)$ is in \$/yr, the EVPI is in \$/yr, and the present-value conversion (Eq.~\ref{eq:voi}) produces a present-value total in \$.

\subsubsection{Damage function}

Residual flood damages are computed using the piecewise-linear global damage function of Jevrejeva et~al.~\citeyearpar{Jevrejeva2018}, which maps SLR to annual global flood costs based on estimated asset exposure. For SLR beyond the tabulated range (1.80~m), we extrapolate linearly using the slope of the final segment ($\sim$\$13.8\,T/yr per meter).

\subsection{Two-world structure}

The forecast distribution is decomposed into two conditional distributions, conditioned directly on WAIS scenario membership rather than on a percentile-threshold proxy:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The S1 world} ($S_{\mathrm{S1}}$) samples from the blended forecast conditional on WAIS Scenario 1, representing continued mass loss along the observed trajectory.
\item \textbf{The S2 world} ($S_{\mathrm{S2}}$) samples from the full forecast conditional on WAIS Scenario 2, representing outcomes that allow for rapid WAIS retreat.
\end{itemize}
Because the WAIS mixture comprises exactly these two scenarios, the two worlds partition it completely, but they are built differently. Every Monte Carlo sample's WAIS scenario assignment is recorded at the point of sampling and carried through the component-summation and rate-space-blending pipeline as a per-sample label, and $S_{\mathrm{S2}}$ is the 1{,}805 samples of the blended forecast carrying the S2 label, an exact conditional subset rather than a percentile-threshold proxy. $S_{\mathrm{S1}}$ is not the 195 samples carrying the S1 label, which would be too few to resolve the tail; it is a separate 2{,}000-member ensemble in which WAIS is replaced by its S1 trajectory for every sample, with the non-WAIS components unchanged. The two branches therefore have different sample sizes, 2{,}000 and 1{,}805.

The VoI takes these two conditional worlds as its inputs and recombines them below with the prior $p_s$ as the weight, so the slow- and fast-WAIS cases of the main text, at $p(\mathrm{S1}) = 0.90$ and $0.10$, are mixtures of these worlds rather than inputs to the calculation. The current prior probability of the S1 world is $p_s = 0.10$, the scenario weight of the fast-WAIS case presented in the main text.

\subsection{EVPI calculation}

For a given prior $p_s \equiv p(\mathrm{S1})$ on the S1 world, the EVPI is the annual benefit of learning which scenario holds before choosing the defense height for coastal development.

\subsubsection{Hedging cost (no information)}

Without additional information, the planner hedges by choosing the single defense height $d^*_{\mathrm{hedge}}$ that minimizes the prior-weighted expected cost:
\begin{equation}
C^*_{\mathrm{hedge}} = \min_{d}\; \bigl[\, p_s \cdot C_{\mathrm{S1}}(d) + (1 - p_s) \cdot C_{\mathrm{S2}}(d) \,\bigr],
\label{eq:hedge}
\end{equation}
where $C_{\mathrm{S1}}(d) = C_{\mathrm{adapt}}(d) + \mathbb{E}[D(\max(S_{\mathrm{S1}} - d, 0))]$ and $C_{\mathrm{S2}}(d)$ is defined analogously using $S_{\mathrm{S2}}$.

\subsubsection{Perfect information cost}

With perfect information, the planner would tailor the defense height to whichever world is realized:
\begin{equation}
C^*_{\mathrm{perfect}} = p_s \cdot \min_{d}\, C_{\mathrm{S1}}(d) \;+\; (1 - p_s) \cdot \min_{d}\, C_{\mathrm{S2}}(d).
\label{eq:perfect}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Annual EVPI}

The annual value of perfect information is the difference between the hedging cost and the perfect-information cost:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{EVPI}_{\mathrm{ann}} = C^*_{\mathrm{hedge}} - C^*_{\mathrm{perfect}}.
\label{eq:evsi}
\end{equation}
This is the annual cost penalty of having to compromise on a single design rather than tailoring to the true state. It is non-negative by construction, because $C^*_{\mathrm{hedge}}$ minimizes a weighted sum of the two cost curves while $C^*_{\mathrm{perfect}}$ minimizes each of them separately. As a minimum over $d$ of functions affine in $p_s$, $C^*_{\mathrm{hedge}}$ is concave in $p_s$, and subtracting the affine $C^*_{\mathrm{perfect}}$ leaves a concave function that vanishes at $p_s = 0$ and $p_s = 1$, so the maximum is interior. Its location is set by the geometry of the two cost curves and need not be at $p_s = 0.5$. Here the two worlds are strongly asymmetric, with optimal designs of 0.90\,m under S1 against 2.85\,m under S2, and the maximum falls at $p_s = 0.81$.

\subsection{Discounting and the VoI surface}

The annual EVPI is converted to a present value at the year of arrival by discounting the stream of benefits from the year information arrives ($t_a$) to the target year ($t_T = 2100$) at a real discount rate $r = 3\%$:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{VoI}(t_a, p_s) = \mathrm{EVPI}_{\mathrm{ann}}(p_s)\,\max\!\left(0,\; \frac{1 - (1+r)^{-(t_T - t_a)}}{r}\right).
\label{eq:voi}
\end{equation}
The annuity factor $[1 - (1+r)^{-n}]/r$ captures the number of discounted years over which the improved decision yields benefits.

The VoI surface (Fig.~10c of the main text) is computed over a grid of arrival years ($t_a \in [2026, 2150]$) and prior probabilities ($p_s \in [0.01, 0.99]$). All calculations use the 3$\,^\circ$C (SSP2-4.5) warming trajectory, the scenario closest to current policies \citep{Hausfather2025}. The choice of warming trajectory has qualitatively little impact on the results given all the uncertainties involved. Note that our cost estimates are likely to be underestimates (see \S\ref{s:adaptation_cost}), but the takeaway message remains even if the true costs or damages are an order of magnitude lower. The main text quotes the maximum of this surface over both axes as about US\$4 trillion. The exact maximum is US\$4.3\,T, attained at $p_s = 0.81$ for information arriving in 2026. It is not the value at any single prior. At the $p(\mathrm{S1}) = 0.90$ case adopted in the main text the surface gives US\$3.7\,T, and at $p_s = 0.10$, the prior marked on the panel, it gives US\$0.8\,T. 

\subsection{Key assumptions and limitations}

The VoI calculation is intended to be simple analysis to make a simple point, not to stand for detailed financial decisions. As such, we make several simplifying assumptions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Binary resolution.} Information perfectly resolves the slow/fast dichotomy. Partial information (e.g., updating $p_s$ from 0.10 to 0.30 without full resolution) would yield lower VoI.
\item \textbf{Single decision.} The planner makes one irreversible design choice. In practice, adaptive management with staged decisions would reduce the hedging penalty, lowering VoI.
\item \textbf{Global aggregation.} Costs are aggregated globally. Local VoI would differ based on coastal exposure and elevation.
\item \textbf{Linear adaptation cost.} We assume $C_{\mathrm{adapt}}$ scales linearly with defense height. Economies or diseconomies of scale would modify the optimal designs.
\item \textbf{Discount rate.} The 3\% real discount rate is standard for infrastructure but lower rates (reflecting intergenerational equity) would increase VoI. Higher discount rates, that would lower VoI, are unlikely given the long timeline of risk exposure from sea level rise. 
\end{enumerate}

Despite these simplifications, the analysis provides a rough order-of-magnitude estimate of the economic value of resolving WAIS uncertainty, supporting the argument that targeted investment in ice-sheet observations and process understanding has large expected returns.
